No one dares live in it. A neighbor once heard “nisten und gummern” there throughout an entire night, as if many old documents and parchments were being unrolled, read, and thrown about. A certain Jost from Mörel and a Joseph Kummer from Ried acquired their “lahmen Tag” in this house. The latter once saw a great bird of prey fluttering about the open entryway in broad daylight. He himself told me that on 17 August 1842 he had made an excursion to the beautiful Mörjeralpe and, because the day had grown too short, was invited by Mr. Chaplain Benetz to stay overnight. Since there was no other place, Benetz prepared a bed for him in the Bozenstube—he himself occupied the uppermost floor of the same house—and after a walk, supper, and a much-enjoyed game of Troggenspiel, led him down into it around eleven o’clock at night. Mr. Mooser knew nothing of the Bozen haunting and was well satisfied with the assigned lodging. To reach the room they passed through the entryway and kitchen, where all manner of household clutter lay about in disorder. In the dwelling itself he found bare walls and benches and two old bedsteads, one empty and the other made ready for him. Before it stood a large chest used as a broad step up to the high bed. Suspecting nothing bad, tired and sleepy, he was just about to fall peacefully asleep when a noise in the kitchen awakened him. He thought the house door had not been properly closed and that a stray Alpine goat was seeking shelter. But soon he heard the room door open with a forceful jerk, something enter, and a regular knocking in three different tones go around the room. Still he believed he was hearing something natural and, as a priest, wished neither to believe in Bozen nor to fear them. He therefore tried to fall asleep again. Then his head together with the pillow was gently lifted while the knocking continued. He still thought it a play of imagination and again tried to sleep. He and the pillow were lifted a second time, higher still, and it knocked again. Before long he was lifted with the pillow a third time, now with such force that his upper body rose almost upright and fell back into bed with a crash. That was too much after all. The frightened gentleman now sat up in bed, stared wide-eyed around the dark room for the ghost, and began to pray. The knocking continued, but more softly. The good man was entirely at a loss over what to do. He nevertheless resolved to endure it because he was ashamed to flee. He therefore lay down again, this time with his face turned toward the wall. Then he heard a tremendous leap onto the bed chest and felt the ghost beside him in the bed, threatening to crush him. It seemed as though a fiery pig were piercing his back to the innermost marrow with glowing bristles. Then it seized him so violently by the neck that he would have cried aloud in pain had he been able to draw breath. He thought he was dying and inwardly commended himself to God and the Mother of God. And behold! In an instant the ghost was gone and he was free again. With a single leap he sprang loudly crying from the bed, gathered up his clothes, took his shoes in hand, and sought the open air. It may have been around twelve o’clock. He spent the remainder of the night in a warm goat stable and in the morning found five blue marks on his neck, clear impressions of a rough five-fingered hand. Whoever will not believe the story should ask the man so sorely tried himself; he is still alive.
