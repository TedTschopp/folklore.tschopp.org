At the end of the lower village there still stands a high tower with the steep curtain wall of the castle of the knight of Falkenstein; by the upper village, in a little wood close beside the Aare, lie the ruins of the robber castle Hagnau. A small fir has taken root at the top of this castle tower, and precisely beside this little tree, whenever the weather changes, the figure of the notorious castle lord can be seen. Then at night he comes down to the bank, strides back and forth spraying sparks, and calls across the water. Despite the roar with which the river passes here over crags and gravel bars, his clear voice can be heard distinctly at the Steckhof on the opposite side, an isolated farmstead that likewise took its name from Hagnau Castle. Everyone in the surrounding country and on both banks of the river, in the village of Däniken as well as in Ober-Gösgen across the water, knows something to tell of this castle spirit, and all alike call him the Schlossgrün. People everywhere also still speak of the marvelous disappearance of a young man from the local Steckhof who recently became involved with this Schlossgrün. We heard the following from the young man himself. Since he is reserved and avoids people, and his words by no means come freely, there is at least little reason to suppose that he intended his account to exploit his listener’s credulity.", "In the late autumn of 1856, a sudden cry for help once woke me from sleep at midnight. When I opened the sliding window to listen, the voice came clearly from the little Hagnau wood, which lies not a hundred paces from our three houses and the road. Wind and rain struck my face at the same time, but I pitied the poor man who must have lost his way in such bad weather and so close to our farm. Without hesitation, therefore, I left my dear bed and went into the little wood. But, O heaven, what a terrifying figure stood before me! There was a man as tall as a tree, enveloped from head to foot in a broad cloak, and when one looked up toward his head, two enormously large fiery eyes rolled about in a black face. With all my strength I tried to take to my heels, but already it was impossible. For now the Black One strode forward and drew me into his course. Behind him I had to place each footstep in his, through thick and thin, over stump and stone, across cultivated and uncultivated ground. In a broad arc he wandered across the full width of our fields. As we approached the village and perceived the first houses through the darkness, I tried with all my might to leap beneath one of the roofs. But in vain; already we were going farther, onto the Eicher-Almende, then through the tract of woods called Lehen, far beyond our village bounds, and then all the way across into the forests of the village of Köllikon. Without any will of my own, I was dragged away from the Aare valley across the wooded heights and into the valley beyond. Rain poured from above and the streams swelled below. I had to wade through them one after another; I dripped with wet, and my hands and feet were already bleeding, torn by thorns and pathless crags. Wordlessly my leader strode on, and I followed in the dense darkness of his form. Now we approached a farmhouse lying close against the edge of the Köllikon forest. Into it the terrible being rushed amid thunderous cracking and crashing. I cannot say what became of me. But when I returned to my senses, I found myself hidden in the hay of a shed some distance from that house. I had lain asleep here into the second day when tormenting hunger woke me. I sought out the nearest house and asked the people for a piece of bread. But in terror they threw a piece out to me and hurriedly closed the window. Only from their looks and speech did I grasp my own appearance: all my clothes torn away, arms and legs bloodied, eyes red with feverish heat, and a head swollen to the size of a Kornmetzen—such was the figure I presented. When I came home, they scarcely recognized me. No one understood my long absence. Namely, he honored the castle spirit with an invitation couched in very indecent terms and, to make himself fully understood, illustrated it by dropping his trousers.
