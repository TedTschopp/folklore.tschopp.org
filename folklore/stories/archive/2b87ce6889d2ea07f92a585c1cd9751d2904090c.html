Hans and Jakob lived closest together; Joachim, the third, lived somewhat farther away in the Mühlimatte, at the place now called the High Cross. As frugally as the first two lived, so grandly and arrogantly did Joachim, the eldest, behave. He had no desire to farm, opened a tavern, and in his conceit took great pride in the honor the people of Bremgarten showed him by diligently drinking his wine. In his lordly arrogance, he would speak contemptuously of rural trades, and once, before his guests, he boasted that his farm, together with its common land and forest, was for sale to any townsman who could pay him a Metzen of Zurich Angster within three hours. Angster was the name given to silver pennies stamped with the severed heads of Felix and Regula, the patron saints of the city of Zurich. During this conversation, the Bremgarten councilmen continued drinking vigorously until the drunken innkeeper’s eyes closed. They then hurriedly sent a rider to Zurich to fetch them a bushel of Angster from the mint there, and at the same time they turned back the hand of the clock in the room. When Joachim awoke in the meantime and had returned to better judgment, he regretted his word and would gladly have undone the hasty bargain. But now the returning messenger entered the tavern and emptied the agreed bushel full of Angster before him. Since it was too late, Joachim finally asked only for the right, at any time and as often as necessary, to fell as much timber in the sold forest as the three brothers might in future require for their four houses and two barns in Fischbach if they lost them by fire or otherwise had to demolish them because they had become unsafe. The cunning councilmen granted this on the condition that these houses must always be rebuilt on their old sites. Bremgarten thereby gained a double advantage. Since the sold forest had until then remained undivided among the three brothers, the shares of the other two had now also been wagered away to the town. At the same time, all the descendants of the three brothers were excluded from the agreed use of the forest as soon as they no longer found room in those four dwellings and had to build on other parts of their lost inheritance. This soon happened. The Seiler family in Fischbach now constitutes two-thirds of the population there and, for its greatly increased numbers, has no privilege beyond two Juchart of municipal forest except the right to that building timber which Bremgarten must supply for the four houses and two barns for as long as, under the agreement, they are renewed on the old sites and within the same space. The houses still stand, and a document recording the agreement is in the possession of the Seiler family. Some time ago, Bremgarten also tried to free itself from this final obligation and offered, in exchange for surrendering the document, to transfer to the village a forest of about forty Juchart. As tempting as this gift was to a village poor in timber, the farmers refused to be caught a third time. They rejected the forest because it lies entirely beside the water and is increasingly undermined and carried away each year by the wild Reuss.
