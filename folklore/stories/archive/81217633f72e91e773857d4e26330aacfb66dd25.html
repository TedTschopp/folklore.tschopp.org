Some of these lie quite level and horizontal, which is why the place has been called Z'en-Tischen. According to an old legend, a great treasure was once hidden there. Before sunset, people are often said to have seen heaps of silverware gleaming upon these Blatten and a noblewoman sitting beside them, yet no one dared approach this apparition. Once, however, a poor shepherd was passing near the place at sunset when he saw the gray Blatten of Z'en-Tischen covered with snow-white cloths upon which heaps of silver objects shone brightly. At one side he saw a noble young woman in old-fashioned dress, sitting motionless as a tombstone beside these valuables. Her head rested upon one arm, and she veiled her face with a little white cloth that she held in her hand. With the other hand, she beckoned him closer. Although an involuntary horror came over him, he slowly followed her summons. He was already so close that he could see numerous gemstones upon her fingers, neck, and breast, flashing and shimmering now blue, now red, now yellow, like dewdrops on flowers in the morning sun. But the more beautiful the countess appeared to him in her rich old dress, and the more brilliantly the treasures shone toward him, the more afraid he became. He had drawn so near that he was about to ask her, “Gracious Countess, what is your desire?” when his heart began to pound so violently with dread that the words died upon his ribs. He turned and fled as quickly as he could. Just then the sun went down, and behind him came thunder and crashing as though a mountain were collapsing. Fear drove him so hard that he did not even dare look back. But remorse soon followed this childish fear. “Foolish idiot,” he said, striking his forehead in vexation, “you have fled from your own good fortune.” He reproached himself bitterly and spent a sleepless night. “Tomorrow,” he said resentfully of his childish fear, “I will make good my mistake.” He truly set out, and on his way he painted the apparition he had seen in the rosiest colors in his imagination. He pictured how humbly he would beg the noble woman's pardon for his flight and ingratitude if she beckoned to him again; in short, he already believed that the countess would make him heir to her riches. During this soliloquy, the sun sank toward the horizon as it had the day before, and he stood close to the place where he had witnessed so splendid an apparition. Today, however, it was different: he saw only the fissured gray rocks. In vain he stood motionless for some time, as though lost in a dream, waiting for the countess to appear to him with her riches. Everything around him was as quiet as a mouse. There were only the split gray Blatten, and no apparition appeared again. Then, in the warm rays of the setting sun, a magnificent butterfly suddenly fluttered around the broken rocks and toward him. He tried to catch it and already thought he had seized it, but it slipped from his hand, flew toward the fissures in the rock, and vanished between them just as the sun went down. A warm current of air brushed him, and it seemed to him as though someone whispered in his ear, “You already held good fortune in your hand; why did you turn your back on it?” How often he later tried to make good his mistake, reconcile himself with the offended lady, and beg her forgiveness upon his knees.
