Among other things, he could ‘gstellen’ livestock and people—that is, prevent them from carrying out some undertaking, whether good or evil. He possessed the famous but rare Strüdelbücher, which teach the magical arts. For that reason, his body is said to have turned charred black after his death. Once a poor little farmer from Gsteigboden came to Strüdeljaggi and asked him to gstellen the rogues who plundered his cherry trees every night. ‘Find a horseshoe,’ the wonder-doctor snapped at him, ‘but one with seven holes in it, no more and no fewer. Take the iron and bury it at midnight at the crossroads leading to your trees.’ The little farmer was frightened and wanted nothing to do with the affair, but asked the doctor to help him in his loss. At last the doctor promised, and the little man slipped away. During the night—it happened to be Saturday night—Strüdeljaggi stood in the Gsteigboden and waited for the Nachtbuben. Scarcely had they climbed the tree when he spoke his formula. The rascals were spellbound and could not move a limb. When people went to the sermon on Sunday morning, the two were still in the cherry trees to their own disgrace. The wonder-worker then said, ‘If you do not hand over the last of last year's meat, the Strüdel is stronger than we are, and you, your wife, and your children will have to pay.’ That put speed into the man. He invited the doctor to fetch at once by sled everything hanging in the smoke. The doctor immediately agreed. The bounty seemed endless: seven Hammen, four sides of bacon, and an entire chain of sausages appeared. The doctor quickly took possession of them in order to stop the Strüdeln's work.
