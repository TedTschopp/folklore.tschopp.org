The people called her only the Spinner or the Humble, as is still written today beneath her image upon the wall of the old church at Payerne. Both names belonged to her by right. She neither lived in a magnificent palace, nor was surrounded by a splendid court, nor adorned herself with jewels and golden garments as the queens of our own day are accustomed to do. Simple and humble as the housewife of whom Holy Scripture says, “She reaches her hand toward the distaff and her fingers grasp the spindle; she opens her arms to the poor and reaches her hands to the needy,” she traveled through her kingdom upon her palfrey, spinning with the distaff before her. She spent the night now here, now there, at a farm or upon one of her manorial estates, whose yield she knew with the greatest precision down to the eggs in the henhouse. A true mother of the people, she did more on such journeys than inquire into the welfare and suffering of her subjects. Wherever she saw idleness or negligent conduct, she drove the people with maternal strictness toward work and more vigorous action. Prosperity increased throughout the land, and the duties and taxes needed for its flourishing—which Queen Bertha apportioned according to its yield—could be met without difficulty or hardship.", "The inhabitants of Mont above Lausanne still tell of the ever-wandering Bertha, although not without some resentment. Whenever she stopped before a farmhouse, they say, she asked whether her horse had been given oats or wheat so that she could learn the soil's yield and tax it according to its produce. The cultivation of wasteland, repair of roads, foundation of towns, establishment of schools and monasteries, and creation of refuges for the poor and sick were the works of the good and devout queen. Having planted the seed of a new civilized society and become the providence of the fatherland, she also became its shield and defense. By building strong fortifications upon its borders, she knew how to protect it from invasions by foreign hordes, Hungarians, and Saracens. On many a hill from the Alps down to the Jura, one can still see defensive works linked with Queen Bertha's name and the legend that accompanies her.", "One of them is the Tower of Gourze upon a spur of the Jorat not far from Cully. Every winter, when damp mists rise from the wet ground and settle upon the mountain slopes, she appears above its gray masonry in a luminous white garment and scatters seed for a rich harvest from a full winnowing basket. Later, at Christmastime on holy Christmas night, she ranges through her kingdom from that place as a huntress, again dressed in shining white and carrying a magic wand. An airy band of mischievous spirits—male and female elves—accompanies her. As in her lifetime, she stops before every house and every farm to inspect its condition. Woe to the place where she does not find everything in order: where flax still lies unspun, the attic has not been aired, the cellar has not been protected against invading cold, the linen in chests and cupboards is in disorder, food and provisions are not stored in their proper places, dust and dirt have gathered beneath the stairs and in the corners, the livestock in the stalls has not been tended, negligent farmhands and lazy maids lie dirty and uncombed in unmade beds, wicked children make their parents' old age bitter, or the old discipline and custom have perhaps disappeared entirely. There, as a witness to her visit, she is certain to leave behind a punishing or admonitory sign: a warning to use the new year's time better, to be attentive, diligent, and active, and to turn back from the path of evil toward the good. Her sign is more or less severe according to the blameworthiness of what she found. Sometimes the unspun flax is tangled into an inextricable ball; sometimes the attic hatches are torn from their hinges; sometimes the potatoes in the cellar are frozen; sometimes the linen in the chests and cupboards turns musty; sometimes the provisions spoil; sometimes the dirt behind the doors and in the corners is scattered throughout the whole house. At other times, the livestock screams and bellows in the stable until the farmhands and maids awaken. Their bedcovers torn from their bodies, they start up and rush shivering and trembling with cold toward the stalls, believing that a marten has got among the chickens or pigeons, or that the bull or a stallion has broken loose. Yet none of this has happened. By the dim light of the stable lantern, maids and farmhands stare at one another and ask in terror, “What was that?” Resounding laughter answers the question.
