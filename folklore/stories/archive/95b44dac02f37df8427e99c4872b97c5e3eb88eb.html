They swarmed and whirred through the wilderness like partridges or guinea fowl. Because these birds are called Biberli in children’s speech, the dwarfs were called Erdbiberli. When they wished to go among people, however, they first laid aside their bird form. Otherwise, they could not have helped so nimbly in house and field, as they did in the village of Oeschgen or for the farmer on the Kinzhalde, whom they helped each year with the grain harvest. In payment for their helpfulness, he left two sheaves standing for them in every field. From these they baked brown, hard gingerbread dotted all over with little holes; even now, these cakes are called Biberzelten after the name of their inventors. The most famous cakes of this kind were baked by those who lived nearest the town of Laufenburg in a forest cave south of Habsburg-Laufenburg Castle. Here they had their woodland monastery, and everything within it followed the monastic rules exactly and completely. While some had to pray and conduct the church service, others performed kitchen duty. From the neighboring village of Kaisten, people could sometimes watch their activities. Some held a field procession, walking in vestments that reached down to their feet. Others, who kept house in the meantime, wore white pointed caps.
