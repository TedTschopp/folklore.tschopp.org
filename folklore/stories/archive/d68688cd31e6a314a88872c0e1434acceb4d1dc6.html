Not only did it have no fear of the hunters' pursuit; on the contrary, it seemed to provoke them further, well aware that they could do it no harm. When the hunters came close and were about to fire, it merely turned around and watched quite calmly as the powder fizzled in the priming pan. No shot intended for it would go off, and the fruitless hunt for the wolf continued for a long time. Then a Tyrolean arrived. A man who had often hunted the wolf in vain showed him his gun, complained of the vexation, and said he thought his firearm was no good. The Tyrolean, a master of “Verstellen,” “Nestelknüpfen,” and other arts, examined the weapon. “Der Flinte fehlt nix, nur der Schuss ist verstellt”—“Nothing is wrong with the gun; only the shot has been bewitched.” He drew the old charge, asked for three grains of barley, loaded them with the powder, and warned the hunter that when he saw the wolf again he should aim as well as possible but not hold the gun too close to his body when pulling the trigger, for this time the shot would go off powerfully. Sure enough, the wolf returned; the hunter took aim and fired. The discharge was so powerful that it gave the good man a “grausame Täsche”—a terrible blow—and blasted off the gunlock. The next morning he went to where the wolf had stood and found in the snow a tuft of human hair and several drops of blood, as well as wolf tracks, which he followed up to Pany. An old woman had lived there for some time who was reputed to be a witch. He sought her out and found her ill in bed with her face bandaged. When she saw him coming, she raised such a clamor and lamentation that the hunter became “wind und weh”—sick and faint—and was glad to escape her presence.
