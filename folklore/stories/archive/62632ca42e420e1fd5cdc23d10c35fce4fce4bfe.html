Buss" ], "english_paragraphs": [ "Fahrende Schüler looked through the Bergspiegel into the rock wall and discovered immeasurable treasures of gold and silver there, as they did at the higher Steienwand and in the Goldloch of nearby Alp Sennis. For the Venediger, the stones of this region turned into gold, and they are said to have drawn all their wealth from it. In the smooth rock wall between the chapels, inaccessible to ordinary people, is a door leading into the mountain to the treasures. At times it opens in broad daylight. Clergymen come out, climb the rock wall to the summit—one cannot understand how—and there air their chasubles and all kinds of precious implements between the chapels. During this time, no one dared climb to the height. When they have aired their treasures long enough, they return, vanish into the mountain, and the door closes again. At night, however, far more uncanny things take place on the height. Often, when the bells in Flums or Bärschis have struck midnight and the valley lies in deepest darkness, it suddenly grows light above. Black figures can be seen playing skittles between the two chapels. With two golden balls, they tirelessly throw at silver pins. Down below, one distinctly hears the dull rolling of the balls, the fall of the struck pins, and the confused voices of men until, at the stroke of one, everything suddenly falls silent and vanishes. Some of the black figures wear white head coverings, but on most no white can be seen any longer. The former can still be released; the latter cannot.\nO. Giger.", "***", "Wodan, god of the winds, is also god of active nocturnal play. In Christian times Saint George, patron of knights and wealthy lords, frequently took his place. Indeed, the noteworthy old pilgrimage chapel is dedicated to Saint George, and the noisy following of the former heathen god became a host of restless spirits. Their haunting still fills the regions where their lord and master was once most zealously worshiped. Saint Georgenberg is without doubt an ancient cult site. It was a natural sacrificial altar for heathen feasts; a Roman fort also stood upon it, followed in the Middle Ages by a small Beguinenkloster. The voice of the people has preserved all of this in its legends down to our own time.\nAfter Dr. E.
