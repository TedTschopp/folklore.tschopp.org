It would not accept Zieger or cheese. Many people generously gave it Anken, and it put all of it into a tin that was not at all large. People could not understand how it managed to fit everything inside. Once, children watched as it stuffed Anken into the tin. It had a cord and made all sorts of movements, so that the children said, ‘Look, he is making sausages!’ The children asked where the Anken was going. It was going straight to Montefun, the figure said. Strangely, it never spent the night anywhere. When old Stetter of Spiringen invited it to stay with him—he intended to question it closely—it refused, saying that it had a house and home of its own. On the Laue Alp, the people would give it no Anken. It said it would remember them for that. It left; the fine, clear weather continued for a while, and the people were already saying that the Bettelmandeli had forgotten them. But on the third or fourth day, an unheard-of hailstorm swept over Laue and the surrounding area, destroyed all the grass, and stripped even the fir trees in the forests so bare that they looked like Geschner. At last, the people threw scythes out in front of the hut, and the storm immediately subsided and the hail stopped. But they heard a jubilant cry on the Spitzä. They thought it was the Bettelmandeli. Later, it wandered through the Oberland and boasted there about the trick it had played on the people of the Schächental.
