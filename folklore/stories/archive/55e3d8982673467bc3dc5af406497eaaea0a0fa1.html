His name was Walther, but he had called himself Johannes after Christ's disciple ever since he and other nobles vowed to take up the cross and make a pilgrimage to the Holy Sepulcher. Although the old man did not like to see his heir depart, he had to allow it. As a token that Johannes was the sole heir and successor to the lordship, however, he drew a gold ring from his finger and broke it in two before all the servants. He gave one half to his confessor, who was someday to execute the will, and handed the other half to his departing son. Upon returning, the son was to use it to prove his claims before everyone and reject any unfounded claims made by strangers. Twenty years passed. The old man had long been dead, and no news of the son had ever come. No one remained of the entire noble family except a greedy uncle, the wealthy abbot of the neighboring Muri foundation. The abbot therefore took possession of the castle and quickly transformed it into a monastery. The monks had long been feasting there upon the great pike of Lake Hallwil and the noble game of the high forests on the Homberg when, one day, a foreign knight knocked at the monastery gate. When first asked what he wanted, he named himself Hallwil. The terrified porter ran to the Großkellner, the Großkellner to all the fathers and brothers, and finally the entire convent ran for the famous half-ring delivered to them by the deceased count's confessor as proof of their ownership. Down they went with it before the gate, where the knight was holding the other half before the dismayed monks' eyes. The pieces were fitted together and appeared cast as one. But the prior immediately appeared as well, triumphantly carrying a third half of that ring upon his hand. “What is this commotion?” he cried. “Our complete ring, in its two halves, has long been here in the keeping of our sacristy. Long ago, a pilgrim brought us this piece to join the count's part, and he brought it from the hand of poor Johannes himself, who died of plague in the Orient. With his dying breath, Johannes gave us his castle so that we might pray there for his soul and earn him forgiveness of his sins. Out with this shameless impostor who disturbs the founder's grave-rest and our brothers' devotions!” Thus spoke the prior, and the monastery gates slammed shut. Like a beggar, the young count stood outside before his inheritance. What law could have guarded against such cunning, what judgment could have untangled such snares? Only a Gottesurteil could decide the matter. Johannes summoned the monks to the lists before Aarau and challenged them to single combat. The monastery's Schirmvogt, the knight of Rüßegg, appeared on their behalf. Under the escort of the Free Lord of Mülinen, the armored Johannes rode into the lists, while the Abbot of Kappel presided over the court. The combat took place outside the town beneath the great linden tree on the old bank of the Aare, before sworn witnesses and all the people. The knight of Rüßegg was struck from his horse and killed; as he died, he confirmed his opponent's right. The inheritance-stealing monks had to surrender their spoils. Johannes took possession of his castle, and his line still possesses it. This is said to have happened in 1272. Two centuries later, his grandson saved all Switzerland through his victory in the Battle of Murten." ], "notes": [ "Swiss term “Großkellner”: the senior cellarer or provisioning officer of a monastery, an important administrative office rather than an ordinary dining-room waiter; the frightened porter summons him first.", "Swiss term “Gottesurteil”: literally a ‘judgment of God,’ a trial by ordeal believed to let divine justice reveal the rightful party; here the judicial duel decides the inheritance claim.", "Swiss term “Schirmvogt”: a secular protector and legal or military advocate of an ecclesiastical institution; the knight of Rüßegg fights on the monastery's behalf." ] }, "legacy_urls": [] } ---
