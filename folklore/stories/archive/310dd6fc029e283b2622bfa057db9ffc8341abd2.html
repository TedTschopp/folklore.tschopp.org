If they suffered any misfortune during the year, the wicked Senno was to blame; and that God’s blessing had not fled completely had to be credited to the good herder. When the herder wished to pray, the Senno, if he happened to be in a good mood, yawned and slept; but if he was moody and stubborn, he mocked and ranted about it. When the herder prayed over the livestock and made the sign of the cross, the Senno swore, bellowed at the livestock, and consigned everything to all the devils. When the herder performed his devotions morning and evening before an image or crucifix and afterward blessed himself with Wichwasser, the Senno got up and lay down like the cattle, without cross or crook, and scolded the herder as a parson and stupid devil. Indeed, he ridiculed him so much that the herder almost had to conceal his practice and scarcely found time to form a pious intention. When the herder wished to care properly for the milk so that people received what was theirs, the Senno carried on without worry or conscience. He wanted only to eat and drink well, cook and make little cakes, skim off the cream, use the best provisions, and idle—as though he had only a belly and no soul. In short, in word and deed he followed only his evil nature. He had even made a pact with the Evil One—God preserve us from it!—promising to surrender himself body and soul if the Devil would only let him obtain throughout the summer whatever his appetites desired. What follows allows one to infer what he desired. “Idleness is the beginning of every vice!” All the good herder’s warnings accomplished nothing. One evening the Senno uttered the most dreadful wishes. The Evil One—God preserve us from it!—had good ears; this time he had not been called in vain, and the Senno had offered him his own skin. A terrible storm arose. The wind struck every beam and door open and shut and whistled through every crack as though a troop of cats were yowling; lightning cast fire, thunder cracked so fearfully that it made one shudder, and rain fell as though it were being poured out in tubs. Suddenly the storm tore open the hut door and—Jesus, Mary, and Joseph!—the herder cried, “What is that?” In the middle of the open doorway stood a young and strangely dressed woman. Behind her it flashed so strongly that she seemed to stand in naked fire, and then one thunderclap followed another until the ground trembled. It was a dreadful entrance that ought to have served as a warning, but, dear God, it made no impression upon him. While the herder prayed and his legs moved like a treadle from terror, the Senno, bold as you please, went joyfully to meet her as though she were an acquaintance and led her cordially into the hut toward the fire. As for the herder, he felt now cold and now hot and fled out of her way as quickly as he could. The Senno always called her his Basi, but to the herder it seemed that she was the living Devil. Though she had such a pretty and alluring form, she had an uncanny face and eyes like glowing coals, especially when she turned toward the herder; yet no witch could have made herself more seductive toward the Senno. “Oh, poor fly, the infernal spider has already caught you and drawn you into her web; you are undoubtedly lost,” the herder thought to himself. “Will she remain here tonight?” the herder asked. “What else?” answered the Senno. From the Senno’s daily behavior, one could tell what sort of being his Basi and nightly companion was. Whenever the herder told him that he should repent while there was still time, for the time was already approaching when his hide must go to the tannery, the Senno laughed in his face and mocked him, telling him to be silent with such parson-talk. At last the final day of the Summerig arrived. On the preceding evening there had been such witches’ weather that no one would have dared drive a dog outside. Yet it was a warning for the Senno: “Do you know who comes in weather like that? The dreadful woman, because she travels in just such weather! And do you know why she has come? And what you promised? Make yourself ready for the journey!” It seemed to the Senno as though someone whispered this into his ear. Just as they were preparing with the others to descend from the Alp—something that had not happened once all summer—his Basi suddenly entered the hut. The Senno’s legs began to tremble, and his face became ash-pale. She said to the herder, “I have no business with you; you may go. But the Senno and I still have accounts to settle; he remains here.” With these words she seized him by the arm, hurled him into the Stubji, and slammed the door behind her. At that same moment a dreadful pounding and hammering arose, with cries of pain and murder. The herder could scarcely move his legs from terror. At last, however, he went and looked through the keyhole in the Stubji door—and Jesus, Mary, and Joseph, what a dreadful thing he saw! Something unspeakable! The Senno lay on the floor nailed down like the crucified Lord. His dreadful Basi sat astride him and, with a great bloody knife, skinned him alive. From her head horns could clearly be seen, and from her toes claws protruded. The Devil wanted the skin that the Senno had promised him so often. As gladly as the herder would have saved him out of pity, terror had left him powerless and fear drove him to flee. He ran and ran until he could no longer hear the lamenting and screaming; and when he looked back, the Basi had spread the bloody skin of her lover—the Senno—across the hut roof.
