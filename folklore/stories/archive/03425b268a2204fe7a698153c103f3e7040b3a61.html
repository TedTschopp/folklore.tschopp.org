Although his many thefts could not be proved, the older he grew, the more they gnawed at his otherwise hardened conscience, and in the end he was found hanged. Those who take their own lives, however, become ghosts. Soon he was dragging his chains rattling through the house, lying like a sack across stairs and doorways so that people had to leap over him, and making a nuisance of himself to everyone. Finally, a Capuchin friar was summoned to deal with this household plague. He banished the spirit into a chest, secured it thoroughly with nails, and took it up beneath the roof ridge into the darkest corner. This helped, but not for long. As soon as the people went back out to the fields in spring and had to leave the small children at home by themselves, the children searched the house all the way up beneath the roof and pulled out the nailed-up chest. Because it was too heavy for them to carry out into the light, they pushed it through an opening in the roof and let it plunge into the courtyard. It burst open below, and the spirit was free again. Although the boys were greatly astonished to find nothing at all inside the chest, they nevertheless cleared the boards out of the way and said nothing about it when the adults came home from the fields that evening. The evening soup was cooked, and everyone sat down at the table. But the deceased master immediately came in through the door as well, clanking the chains that he had wound around his body and legs. He took his old place behind the table, filled his tobacco pipe, and calmly began to smoke. After a while, he left the room again and was not heard from any more that night. But the very next morning, the children lay ill, swelled up, and quickly died. This struck the adults so deeply that they sold the house, left the village, and moved to Spreitenbach.
