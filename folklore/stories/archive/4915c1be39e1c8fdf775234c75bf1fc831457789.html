It has wrongly been woven into the story of the destruction of Neuhabsburg on the lake near Meggen; genuine folk tradition distinguishes two events. The Holy Beech is indeed a splendid tree, sheltering beneath its strong branches an ancient image of the Compassion of Mary. Before the present beech, already very old, stood an earlier one that collapsed from age. The sacred image, visited by people from far away, has itself received shelter and protection. It originated with a pilgrim traveling to Maria Einsiedeln. On the journey in this region, he either lost his way or suffered an accident—I do not know which. In short, he was in distress and vowed that if help came, he would establish a memorial image in Mary’s honor at this place. Thus the image came to the Holy Beech. He is also said to have endowed a holy Mass in the collegiate church at Lucerne. Independent of this, as already stated, is the following event. When the Lucerne forces took Neuhabsburg Castle on the Ramenfluh beside Lake Lucerne, the victors granted the wife of the castle bailiff free departure together with permission to load up her dearest possession. She did what the women of Weinsberg, or the lady of Tengen Castle, had done and thereby saved her husband. But he was ashamed that a woman had rescued and carried him away through cunning, and on the Meggen common pasture, he killed his noble and faithful wife. His murder-stained conscience tormented him unbearably. After wandering for a time, the knight returned to the site of his wicked deed and made an evil end of his life there. This happened several minutes from the Holy Beech, where the little chapel of Saint Onuphrius now stands beside the old Meggen road.
