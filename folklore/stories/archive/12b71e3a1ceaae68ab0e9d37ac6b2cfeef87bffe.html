She prayed a great deal for the poor souls in the Aletsch Glacier. When she sat at her distaff in the long winter nights, spinning diligently by a night-light, she prayed almost constantly for the dead. She left the house door unlocked so that the poor souls could enter her old, heated room and warm themselves. Yet they needed her permission to enter, and she did not grant it until she went to bed. Then she opened a window and called softly outside, ‘Now—but without harming me!’ She left a stub of candle burning and went to bed. Soon the house door and then the room door opened quietly, as though moved by a cool draft. Countless, barely audible footsteps pattered and clattered inside, as though a crowd were pressing into the room and around the warm stove. Toward the ringing of the prayer bell, she heard the same noise passing out through the door again.", "Once, this widow happened to stay up longer than usual and spun industriously; outside, meanwhile, it was very cold. All at once, a voice called distinctly before the window: ‘Schoch’—that is to say, it is cold, or we are freezing—‘the Altschmidja’—as the woman was called—‘is still spinning.’ ‘I know very well,’ she replied. ‘I only want to finish spinning this little tuft of tow.’ But it was not long before the voice called more loudly: ‘Schoch, the Altschmidja is still spinning!’ Then she grew impatient. ‘If you cannot bear it until I am finished, come inside.’ But she forgot to add, ‘without troubling me.’ The house door and room door flew open as if struck by a strong gust of wind, and the footsteps of the invisible evening visitors became so numerous, and the rustling around her lasted so long, that it seemed it would never end. She herself became so terrified that she thought she would suffocate from the heat, and she could not move away from the distaff—so tightly was the room packed with poor souls. She saw it as a punishment for having made the dead wait so long in the cold. From then on, she was more compassionate and more cautious. When the compassionate old Altschmidja was drawing her final breaths and the sickroom attendants said to one another, ‘What will the poor souls call now, when their friend is dead?’ a loud cry sounded before the windows in the stillness of the night: ‘Schoch, the Altschmidja is still alive!’ The dying woman made signs that she rejoiced at this voice, and then gave up her spirit. At that same moment, the attendants saw a bright glow outside the windows. When they looked out, they saw a great procession of burning lights moving from her house to the glacier; when the lights reached it, they went out one after another. ‘Those are the poor souls,’ the attendants said to one another, ‘with the night-lights she used to burn for them; they are accompanying their friend!
