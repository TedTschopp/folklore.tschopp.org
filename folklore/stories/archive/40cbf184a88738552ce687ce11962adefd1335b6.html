From that time onward the ferry was constantly disturbed by a water spirit. Whenever it reached the middle of the river, the water became wild and raging, and unless the boat immediately turned back, it risked being overturned. The crossing was therefore avoided entirely. The ferryman refused them and explained every circumstance thoroughly. It mattered greatly to the monks not to be delayed on their homeward journey that day but to arrive properly at their monastery within the time prescribed for them. They therefore promised the ferryman, by the omnipotence of God, complete safety for his life and pressed him to take up the oar. He obeyed and brought them to the dangerous place in midstream, where the water began to boil and rage. But instead of letting him turn back, they ordered him to hold still and conjured the spirit to appear in their boat. In the form of a long-haired little black dog, it immediately sprang from the Reuss into the boat. Asked who it was and by what right it disturbed navigation there, it explained that it was not the spirit of the unfortunate maiden at all, but the Devil himself. He wanted people to believe that the girl caused the dangerous turmoil in the water, for the longer such a belief endured, the greater the misdeeds malicious people would attribute to her, the more pitilessly they would attack her honor and defile her memory.
