His gigantic figure and long red beard gave him a terrible appearance. He seldom came out of his armor, spent much of his time hunting, and tormented his subjects cruelly. Once, while riding along the left bank of Lake Brienz toward his castle of Iseltwald, he came upon a fisherman’s little hut, built scarcely three months before. The fisherman’s daughter stepped from its door, handsome and lovely, with artfully coiled hair. Burning with desire to possess the beauty, Wolf ordered father and maiden to his Ringgenberg Castle. With a heavy heart, the old man stepped into the little boat with his child on the appointed day and rowed them across to the foot of the lord’s castle. When the Wehrwolf sought to keep his beloved child at the castle, the father resisted with all his strength, and when the knight threatened them, the two fled to regain their home shore in the boat. But before they had pushed off from the bank, an arrow hissed through the air swift as lightning and, missing the fisherman, pierced the heart of his beloved child. The unhappy father returned home. The man struck it upon the masonry so that the sound carried far and wide. Then the old man asked what the castle would be called. “Schadenburg, let him who will take note!” cried the Zwingherr with wild laughter. Now the old man drew himself up tall. A dreadful fire glowed from his eyes.
