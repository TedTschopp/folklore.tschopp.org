She relieved her loneliness by spinning; and if she had formerly been called Altschmidja, the valley people soon called her only the Spinner at the Aletsch. She often prayed for the poor souls in the glacier, and when she spun deep into the night, the murmur of her prayers mingled with the whir of the humming spinning wheel. Every night she left the house door a little open so that the poor souls could enter her warm room and heat themselves beside the great Giltsteinofen. The souls of the Aletsch Glacier, however, were not permitted to come in until the old woman had gone to rest. So that they would know when this had happened, the widow opened a window and called down toward the glacier: “Now—but without harm or injury to me!” Then she left a stub of candle burning and went to bed. Soon the house door and the room door were gently opened as though by a delicate woman’s hand; a cold breath of wind swept in, and then came a tripping and a trampling, as though innumerable people were crowding into the room and toward the Giltsteinofen. All night long they went in and out as in a beehive, but the good widow slept soundly in her bed, with the blanket pulled up to her temples, and saw and heard nothing.
