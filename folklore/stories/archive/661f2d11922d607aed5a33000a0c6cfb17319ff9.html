At the rich farm there, a poor old little man who made his living gathering herbs was allowed to stop for food and lodging once each year. The miserly people did not permit him to come more often. Nor did they ever give him a bed for the night, though two high-piled beds always stood unused. The little herb-gatherer submitted and spent the night in the hayloft. Once again he lay in the hay on the upper floor when the farmer entered the threshing floor below, dragging with him a lantern, a hoe, and a copper kettle. He dug up the floor, lowered the great cheese kettle into the pit, and left again. Soon he returned, brought a sack of thalers, and threw it into the open kettle with the words, ‘You must be buried in drei Teufels Namen!’—‘in three devils’ names!’ The farmer went away and returned three times, casting his sacks into the kettle three times. In the meantime the little herb-gatherer had also descended from the hayloft each time; because he had far too little of what that miser had too much, he took a pocketful of thalers from each sack and hid himself beneath the hay again in time. Now the man entered for the fourth time. This time he brought a snow-white little donkey draped with a red cloak. He led it around the kettle three times, repeating his curse just as often: ‘You must be buried in drei Teufels Namen!’ Then he filled in the pit, struck off one of the donkey’s legs in drei Teufels Namen, and dragged the poor animal outside with him. The little herb-gatherer did not sleep much longer after this but left the barn at the earliest hour and went on his way.", "When the appointed time had passed and he came to the house again the next year, he found everything changed. The farmer had already been dead for half a year. His two daughters could speak of nothing but grief and vexation. ‘Since you were last with us, there has been nothing but distress and misery in our house. Father did not have a single healthy day, and when he died, none of the great fortune people believed him to possess could be found. The good acquaintances we once had have stayed away as well. Neither of us can find a husband, and we no longer know what to do.’ Suddenly a light dawned on the little herb-gatherer. If it was nothing more than that, he said, he could surely help them that very night. They need only procure for him, in complete secrecy and without anyone noticing, a donkey and a red cloak. The donkey still stood in the stable, snow-white but with only three legs ever since the farmer had struck off one foot. Without attracting attention, they borrowed the red official cloak from the wife of the village constable. With these they went onto the threshing floor and dug at the old spot, then led the little donkey in the red cloak around the hole once again in drei Teufels Namen—but this time to the left, because the dead farmer had once gone around to the right. And behold, there lay the money in the three sacks. The daughters at once wished to share it with the little herb-gatherer. But he told them what had once happened and how he had already taken his share from it three times. That, of course, had long since been used up. Yet if they would let him reach once more into each sack, he would have quite enough for the rest of his life. The daughters did so, and the little man left them with great thanks. From then on everything in the house went well again. The spirit that had banged about on the threshing floor since the father’s death, and had not been driven away even by many Masses for the dead, now came to rest. With the recovered money, suitors also returned, and soon the two daughters were handsome married women.
