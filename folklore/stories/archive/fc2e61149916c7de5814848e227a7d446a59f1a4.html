This cave divides into passages running in various directions, but their narrowness makes them inaccessible to visitors. In one of the depressions, when the dark space is illuminated with a torch, water can be seen in the background. At times, one hears in it the sound of heavy subterranean drops of water or a roaring like underground currents of air. It was natural that this uncanny phenomenon should occasion all kinds of speculation among uninformed people. More striking still was the way the water at the back of the cave suddenly rises and leaves the belly of the mountain roaring and foaming, then rolls toward the lake in a bed it dug for itself. The water is bright, clear, and fresh, with a specific gravity nearly equal to filtered rainwater. The phenomenon is indeed most remarkable when one sees that it seems to depend upon a whim of the brook whether it thrusts its water from the mountain's womb two or three, or even as many as six times a day, sending it foaming and roaring to the lake. The flow lasts for very unequal periods: often scarcely a quarter of an hour, often for hours, and sometimes even longer; it has frequently been seen flowing for half the day. When it ceases, it does so within a short time. Its bed becomes completely dry again, only to be washed anew by the waves. A dull roaring is said to be heard before it appears and also as it withdraws. These outpourings are by no means regular; they are inseparably connected with the amount of watery precipitation in the atmosphere. When much snow lies on the mountain in winter, a thaw begins, or the Föhnwind blows, the mountain becomes a filter for the melting snow. Water collects in the mountain cave as in an empty barrel. A channel leads the water from it to daylight as through a wine siphon, and the filled space thus seems to empty itself. It goes without saying that the air must play its part.
