It is conducted into a wooden trough or basin beside the chapel. This spring serves the hermits and the honest people who come there for devotion or, as will be related, to bathe. The spring is also called unser Lieben frowen Brunnen, miraculously discovered: a clean and splendidly good drinking water, also commonly das kalte Bad, since many use it in place of a bath, as I myself have seen, though with many misgivings. For it is so cold that one can scarcely keep a hand in it for the length of an Ave Maria. Women and men come from far away to bathe for all kinds of bodily infirmities, without any physicians' advice, and use it as follows. The person must throw himself naked into the spring basin and roll over and submerge three times, body, head, and all, with certain ceremonies prescribed by the hermit. Their faith is so great that the good people believe they are already healed. Indeed, the hermit who was there in this 1601st year, when I visited on the 7th day of August, told me that on the most recently past S. Jackobi des Merern Apostels tag, more than 150 people had been there to use this bath. — As for how this bath—or rather this good spring and place—was discovered, I found the following account in a record kept in the chapel. Yet I could not vouch for every part of it; I took from it only as much as reason dictated and as I believed could pass among informed people, especially because the record bore no authentication. Nevertheless, I compared it with the old tradition and that of the local country people over time, especially in the year 1601, when I again crossed this mountain and visited the place, carefully examining it through continual conversation with the local people, particularly the oldest and most distinguished inhabitants. — The record states that a countryman from Weggis named Barthlome Joler first discovered this gorge with its splendid spring, as is believed, in Ano 1540. After long weariness, he refreshed himself there and, from particular devotion to Our Lady, named the spring unser lieben frauen brunnen. Soon afterward, when he suffered great pain in one arm, he vowed to visit the place with repentance and sorrow for his sins and with promised alms or offering. Out of special devotion, he washed his arm with the spring water and became well. He spread news of this, and many other people with infirmities are said likewise to have washed their afflicted limbs there and recovered their health. — The writer, however, a simple man inexperienced in such matters and well known to me, added something else intended to lend this miracle—if it should indeed be baptized as such—still greater credibility: namely, that this spring is also called Der schwösterbrunnen because three praiseworthy, legitimate sisters born at Küssnacht supposedly lived there and used the spring. The common people declare these sisters holy and believe that they still live bodily but invisibly inside this mountain, along with many other strange circumstances—including pilgrimages by women to such caves and mountain clefts that were at times forbidden—which cannot pass among reasonable people and which I therefore omit. For information drawn from the old tradition alone, however, I will tell who these sisters were and their history. At the time when Emperor Albrecht, born a duke of Austria, forcibly purchased the city of Lucerne and the secular lordship of the lands now called Uri, Schwyz, and Unterwalden from certain foundations and religious houses in Ano 1293, he afterward set certain noblemen over them as governors and Landvögten. These burdened them with intolerable tyranny and willfulness, all of which is true in itself and is related at greater length in the Eydgenössische Chroniken. Now, a pious countryman at Küssnacht below this mountain had three legitimate, fully grown, marriageable, well-formed daughters. The Austrian Landtvogt who then sat in the castle at Küssnacht and governed that region fixed his eye upon them. He ordered his servants to abduct the daughters from a dance and bring them to his castle so that he might abuse them and live out his shameful desires with them. Warned of this, the daughters secretly slipped away from the dance and took the nearest way onto this mountain, where they hid and lived concealed until the land was made safe. This is not wholly unbelievable, although the local chronicles say very little of it. But people add that the sisters live there immortally and sometimes allow themselves to be seen by people, especially descendants of their family, and have spoken and conversed with them, together with more strange circumstances. This should be regarded more as fable than truth, just like the belief of these good, simple people that many Herdlütten, female and male, called pygmies by the ancients, lived in this mountain and indeed were seen within living memory. They kept themselves very private from humans, yet also performed human services for them, attended their weddings and other intimate household feasts and Liechtstubeten, ate and drank with them, and did many similar things that, for the sake of welcome brevity, I gladly leave unmentioned.
