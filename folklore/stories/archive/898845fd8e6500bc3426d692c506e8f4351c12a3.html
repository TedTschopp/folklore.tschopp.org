The weather was so vile that no one would have driven a dog out of the house, and the uncanny night crept through the narrow lanes and corners of the quiet mountain village with its brown little houses. He had already knocked at many a door and asked for lodging for the night, but nowhere had his request been heard. Turned away by the wealthy, he finally went to the home of a small, poor family, wondering whether he might find softer hearts and a modest bed there. And indeed, the kindly poor granted him the hospitality that the hard-hearted rich had refused. ‘We cannot put you in either of the two beds,’ the father of the family told him, ‘but if you are content to sleep on the Ofenbänkli, you may stay the night with us and share our food and drink.’ The weary traveler was content. For three days, the icy north wind drove gray tatters of mist and streams of rain up through the narrow valley. The good people did not send their guest away, however; they faithfully shared food and drink with him, and the Ofenbänkli served as his bed. On the fourth day, the sky finally cleared and the stranger prepared to travel on. As he took leave of the good people who had received him so kindly, he said: ‘I cannot give you any money, but I will leave you something as a reward for your hospitality.’ He took his pocketknife and carved a whole row of letters into the door lintel, whose meaning no one has deciphered to this day. Then he added: ‘This dwelling will come into great danger, but nothing will happen to it.’ And indeed, the people say, one half of the zweimänniges house later burned down; but the other half, where the beggar had been sheltered, was spared by the hungry flames.
