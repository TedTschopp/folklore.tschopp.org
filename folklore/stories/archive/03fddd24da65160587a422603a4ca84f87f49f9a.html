Precisely because he saw that the boy was worth more than he was, he could not stand him; whenever he could burden him with some task and torment him with work beyond the proper time, it suited him perfectly. Once the hut in the upper Staffel had been made habitable, the water channeled into the fountain trough, and the herdsman was about to lie down utterly exhausted on the bed, the Senne ordered him to descend to the lower Staffel and fetch the milking stool. He searched for the milking stool and found it on the table in the main room. Since it was now pitch dark, he no longer dared climb to the upper Alp. He thought he could sleep just as well here and would make sure to be above in good time in the morning. He arranged the straw on the bed and lay down to sleep. He had not slumbered long when the door flew open with a violent jerk. He awoke and saw a whole company of Sennen and Sennerinnen enter. All wore old-fashioned clothes: the men knee breeches and swallowtail coats, the Sennerinnen short colorful skirts, elbow-length sleeves, and the high, stiff Walliserhut. They bustled and ruled as though at home, stirred up the embers, set the cauldron over the fire, carried milk inside in buckets as though they had been milking out in the stable, boiled the milk, and made preparations for cheesemaking. Suddenly two of them brought in a cow, slaughtered it, threw it piece by piece into the cauldron, and boiled it. “Poor Graui,” murmured the herdsman, for he had recognized the animal at once, and the scene filled him with dread. When one of the strange figures approached his bed and asked whether he too desired a drink, the boy felt sick with terror, and cold sweat of fear flowed across his brow. He shook his head and stared at the man in horror. But the spirit said that he must drink whether he wished to or not—and asked which he preferred: fermented, accursed, or rechte Milch, wholesome milk. Very softly he said, “Then give me rechte Milch,” for he had eaten nothing warm that evening. The man set a wooden bowl before him, which he drained in one draught. The milk tasted sweet as honey and gave him strength and courage; now he felt alert and brave, and the doings of the strange nocturnal company interested him. Again one of them approached the bed with a piece of cooked meat that he had just drawn from the cauldron and held out to him. The herdsman thought that if everything had to be eaten, he might as well take part, and he cut himself a little piece. But he was not accustomed to solid fare, and the meat pleased him less than the milk, although magnificent aromas rose from the cauldron. When the cow had been consumed, the bones were thrown into the hide, which was tied into a bundle and carried outside. Then the company began to dance to the sounds of a fiddle and sing in silver-bright voices, so that the herdsman's heart felt entirely at ease and he forgot the horror of his situation. He had never heard such beautiful songs; some voices sounded more beautiful and gentle than those of the teacher, the priest, and all the choir singers in the village church below. Suddenly the entire choir stepped before his bed, and one of the merriest Sennerinnen asked whether he liked the singing.
