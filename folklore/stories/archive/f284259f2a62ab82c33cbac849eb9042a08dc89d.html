The people willingly gave her generous alms of Zieger, Geisskäse, and Anken. In time, however, they noticed that she possessed money and was nowhere near as poor as she pretended. They therefore began refusing her alms. She grew angry and said, ‘Wartet nur! ych wil-i im Nahsummer ä Schwirrä schlah, das-er nu am-mi dänket!’—‘Just wait! In the late summer I shall strike you such a Schwirrä that you will think of nothing but me!’ They had enjoyed a blessed early summer, with grass in abundance, and had stored up plentiful cheese and Anken. Sure enough, scarcely had the late summer begun when, one afternoon, the sky became covered with heavy black storm clouds. In an instant, an indescribable hailstorm discharged over the Brunniboden, such as the people had never experienced. The hail miserably battered the beautiful pasture. Through valleys and gullies, Rübenen and streams roared down toward the Brunniboden. But look! What comes there at the very front of the largest Koosi—a Rübi? It is the beggar woman; she whoops and shouts, sitting at a spinning wheel and spinning. At the very top a second Wybervolch comes riding along, working a reel with all her strength. All the people weep and lament. An old man says, ‘If only someone would run out to Schwanden and ring the chapel bell!’ A strong, fleet-footed boy hears this; he runs as fast as his legs can carry him and sets the little bell of St. Anna’s church ringing.
