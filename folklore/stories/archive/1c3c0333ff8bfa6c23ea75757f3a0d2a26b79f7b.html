For the Goths under King Alaric had invaded Italy and were already threatening the city of Rome. And they helped—Businger says—bravely and powerfully to drive the enemy from the walls of the capital of Christendom. When the people of the Lands, then called Schneeberger, reached the field of battle, their commander recognized that battle and attack would be useless and ruinous for them unless the enemy were vigorously assailed from the other side at the very same moment. He wished to convince the pope’s or emperor’s general of this necessity by letter. To reach the commander in the city, however, the messenger had to swim across a broad river beside the enemy camp. Who was the bold champion who offered himself for this undertaking? A poor young man from Sachseln named Anderhalten. Two letters wrapped in waxed cloth were tied into his long hair; then he set out stealthily. The first letter was for the first Roman sentry, so that he would be allowed to pass, and the other was for the commander. Everything went as desired. He swam back and forth like an otter. The attack was fixed for a moonlit night and carried out. The Lands were victorious.
