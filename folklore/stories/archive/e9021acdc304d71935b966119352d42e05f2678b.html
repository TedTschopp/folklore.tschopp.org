A little farmer whose livestock had all been ruined by a witch said to his wife one morning, ‘If that is how things stand, I too shall become a sorcerer.’ She earnestly advised him against it and warned him with motherly concern against this sinful step. But because he did not abandon his plan and in all seriousness sought out a sorcerer, she finally said, ‘Well, if you are serious, I shall teach you witchcraft myself.’ The man agreed. At midnight, armed with a pitchfork, he had to accompany his wife onto the Miststock. There he was supposed to thrust the fork into the manure heap and renounce God and all the saints. Standing behind him, she recited the formula for him. He murmured something, then suddenly wheeled around like lightning and, with the words ‘Jetz g'seh-n-i, das ä Häx bisch’—‘Now I see that you are a witch’—drove the three-tined pitchfork into the false woman’s heart. In the morning she lay dead on the spot and was charred black. They remained there, and all three subsequently married councilors: one in the Blüemlismatt behind the Reuss, one in Ried above Amsteg, and the third somewhere I do not know. The councilor in Ried soon noticed that his wife could do more than other people and resolved to put her to the test. One day he told her that he too wished to learn her arts. ‘Good,’ she answered. ‘Then come down to Amsteg with me tomorrow evening.
