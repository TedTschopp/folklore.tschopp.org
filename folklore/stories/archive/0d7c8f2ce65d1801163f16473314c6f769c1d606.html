He smashed the stone and put a piece of it in his trouser pocket. That evening, before lying down to sleep in the hay, he stuck it into a Gwätti in the timberwork. Several herdsmen had collected Strahlen at Bergschyen and elsewhere. They had gathered many and kept them in Leonz Baumann's cellar. The cellar was half full of Strahlen, including many large and beautiful pieces. Whenever one entered the cellar, the Strahlen glittered and shone with a beautiful, gentle light. Then a connoisseur who had heard of these Strahlen came to the Alp. He had them shown to him and, once he had seen them, said that he wished to buy only one; what would they ask if he might choose it himself? The people, who knew little about Strahlen, demanded what they considered a fairly large sum. The stranger did not deliberate for long. He paid the price, took one crystal—far from the most beautiful, they believed—and went down the valley. Three poor men from Ried in the parish of Amsteg once climbed toward the Bristenstock to search for Strahlen. At last, after digging for a long time at Bristenstäfeli, they came upon a carbuncle that glittered and shone like the sun. Full of joy and pride, they hurried down to Amsteg, behaved very haughtily over their find, boasted, and invited a large company to join them at the inn, at the upper Tresch. There they placed their carbuncle on the table and said in their godless arrogance: “There now, we no longer need the Lord God's light; this stone gives better light.” In the midst of a bright, clear day, they closed every shutter in the house and feasted, danced, and rejoiced for several days. After a while they went out again—to search for minerals; it seems they still did not have enough. But this time the cave in which they were digging collapsed, buried two of them, and killed them. Only the third man—Hans Ambort, some claim—escaped safe and sound. He had many cattle at Bristenstäfeli—or 10 calves, according to another account—but no longer looked after them. “They can perish for all I care,” he said. “I have enough.” One day, however, a good neighbor brought the cattle right to his house. (Or: one day in autumn they all returned by themselves in the best condition and met him on the Steger Bridge.)
