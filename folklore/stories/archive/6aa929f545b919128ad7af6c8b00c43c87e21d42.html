They left her nothing, however, except a will, which they gave to a good friend to keep until their daughter was grown, and a wonderfully beautiful dress. Since the abandoned orphan otherwise had no means of sustaining her life, she took the dress with her in a little cloth and went in search of service. The girl had to be glad finally to find a place in a very noble household, where she was given the lowliest kitchen and stable work. For that reason, she was called nothing but Aschengrübel. She kept the beautiful garment hidden beneath a fir tree. After some time, there was music and dancing in the village. Foremost among those making merry was the son of the house in which Aschengrübel lived. She had suffered especially much at this son's hands ever since she once proved to him that an impoverished, abandoned girl and strictly chaste conduct were not incompatible things. Now an irresistible desire to go to the dancing place seized her. The heads of the household finally gave her permission to go, since Aschengrübel was willing to be content if she might only watch for a little while. She washed, went to the fir tree, fetched her magnificent dress, and put it on. As though by magic, she now appeared to be a wonderfully beautiful young woman and drew every eye at the dance. The most distinguished of all the young men in particular asked her to dance with him and did not want to let her go. But however urgently he pressed her, she did not give her name and at last managed to slip away on a pretext. Beneath the fir she hid her dress again and returned to the house as she had left it: the poor, unremarkable Aschengrübel. From then on, that young man had lost his peace of heart. Since his search for the beautiful young woman was in vain, he arranged for another dance like the first to be held in the same place, hoping that the lovely one might return. Everything went as it had before: she came. The young man was fated to love her even more, especially when, forgetting himself somewhat, he was given cause to admire the beauty's strict virtue. This incident would very nearly have cost him her favor altogether. Only the sincere self-reflection he showed saved him from the greatest sorrow that could befall him: her dismissing him forever. But she still did not reveal her name to him and again departed as secretly as the first time. When she reached the fir tree, a tiny little man stood there, a dwarf whom she had never seen before. He acted exceedingly friendly and praised her highly for having conducted herself so honorably toward the young man. She should continue in that way, he said, and then she would find happiness. She went home and was what she had been before. She was not a hair better off than earlier, and no one treated her any more kindly. The chastely beautiful young woman, meanwhile, would not leave the young man's mind, and neither comfort nor pleasure helped until there was another dance. He was not disappointed. The one he longed for appeared and seemed to him lovelier still than on the previous occasions. For a long time, she resisted revealing her origins. If he were to know her, or see her as she was during her ordinary work, he would surely no longer love her but despise her, she repeatedly told him. But he gave her the most sacred assurances of undying love and fidelity. And so at last the son of the noble household in which Aschengrübel served learned that this poorest maid of his father's was the bride chosen by his heart. It did not horrify him. He loved her just as much and immediately fixed the wedding day. Aschengrübel made it a condition that until then she should remain unknown and live as before. Her bridegroom had to concede this and promise to keep his bride's name secret. At the fir tree, where the girl intended meanwhile to keep the beautiful garment, the little man stood again. He praised her as before and said that on the wedding day, when she came to fetch the dress, he would see her here once more and give her a gift. What was it that he then handed her? Her parents' will, which made her the heiress to a great lordship. Dressed in the magnificent garment, finer than any other, she came to her waiting bridegroom. He received the will from her and presented the splendid bride to his astonished parents in the person of their own Aschengrübel. Once their union had been blessed and the newlyweds were traveling to the woman's noble estate, the good little man met her again on the road and said that he had one more gift to lay in her apron, especially for her alone. What did he put inside, and bid her watch over carefully? She could not tell her companions for fear of being laughed at, and she let the thing fall unnoticed from her apron, for it was nothing but—Rossbollen. Only a little of it remained stuck to her apron.
