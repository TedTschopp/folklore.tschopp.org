Mathilde endowed a Jahrzeit in the church at Wil.", "Dreibrunnen, as it is now called, was formerly named Tiefenbrunnen (Tüwinbrunnen), or in shortened form Tübrunnen, as it is still pronounced in popular speech today in contrast to the written language. It was a Ketnhof belonging to the Counts of Toggenburg. On it they built a church for the inhabitants of their many surrounding estates and therefore held the patronage of the church at Dreibrunnen. At that time the place must have formed a proper hamlet; today it is a single farmstead with only one dwelling.—To what inhabitant of Wil of my age or older is the name Dreibrunnen not a sweet sound from a devout youth? Who does not gladly remember the hours spent there, whether the after-celebration of first communion, the supplication procession during Rogation Week, or the hand of a praying mother on Sunday pilgrimage led one there—to the little church on the gentle hill, mirrored in the waters of two ponds where swaying reeds bent their spikes and the bordering firs laid down their shadows. What other mysteries those pond-waves once concealed! They were not merely receptacles for fish but the sacred vessel in which the Mother of God watched over and preserved children not yet brought to life, occasionally giving one to an ardently pleading mother. Many a boy claimed that when the water was clear he had distinctly seen the bright blue eyes of a future little brother or sister blinking up from the bottom! Now the ponds have vanished, and with them the magnificent beech wood to the west, which offered benches to the old; skittles, target shooting, drink, and song to the men; and a cheerful playground to the boys and girls. Its majestic trunks and bright, friendly shadows poured poetic consecration or warm feeling over minds of every age, and it often echoed with the voices of happy people and with the song and twitter of merry birds in a common garland of music. The ponds and their myth have vanished; a dreadful sobriety has taken their place, and no children stretch longing little arms toward the light from the depths. The Turbengräber lifts a measured layer of peat from this ground with his shovel; the beech grove was sacrificed to the axe and the wretched return of a few gulden, and in its utter barrenness the hill indicts the emptiness of poetic feeling among the authorities of that time. Only faith in the miraculous power of the local image of the Mother of God has remained among the country people and at least still draws these crowds to the once-celebrated site on fine Sundays.\nAfter C.
