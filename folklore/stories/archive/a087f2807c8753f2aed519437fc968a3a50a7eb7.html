Every Saturday, at the sound of the Betzeitglocke, a mother sow called Moor Moritz runs out leading her ten piglets. She emerges from the part of the little manor that formerly contained the stables but now consists only of a few roofed walls and must serve the community as a woodshed. She makes her way to the Chile-Rainli, a slope belonging to the castle chapel, and from there returns home. She has the coloring of the so-called Zweibrücker breed—black in front and white behind—and her piglets bear the same markings. If a passerby has the courage to approach within a few paces, the sow swells to the size of one of the washing tubs in which slaughtered pigs are scalded and begins to grunt horribly. This means that the person should retreat. One morning, the pig had vanished together with her piglets, and soon the rest of the livestock in the stable began to die. The Junker's fortune dwindled until he gradually sank to the level of a poor Tauner, who had to plow his sole Tagwerk of arable land with another man's oxen. When the roof of his house began to decay, he took the tiles from the chapel roof at night and covered it with straw instead.
