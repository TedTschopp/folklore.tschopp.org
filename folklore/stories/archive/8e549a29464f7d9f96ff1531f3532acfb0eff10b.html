The innkeeper liked the boy and took him on provisionally as a stable hand. There he was called the Züriheiri, as people elsewhere commonly call every Zürichbieter. He showed promise, gradually became a house servant, earned a great deal of money from the large crowds that the Zurzacher Messen then brought, and was finally able to buy a house with his savings and begin trading. But from then on, greed devoured every other inclination in him with a single exception: his faithful friendship with the son of the Ox Inn's proprietor, with whom he had grown up. Even after that son eventually became the town's mayor, this bond did not end, but it was soon to be put to a very hard test. The Züriheiri indulged his greed not only in commerce but also through the most miserly economy in his household. With deep resentment he watched every year as even the least citizen of Zurzach received Gabenholz free of charge from the communal forests, while he alone, a stranger to the town and an Ausbürger, had to go without. Out of sheer defiance, he would not buy wood from a neighbor. He therefore stole it at night, cutting it in the customary manner in the part of the Zurzacherbann called Grüt. The damage had long been noticed, and the Bannwart was ordered to keep a sharper watch. But the lazy watchmen did not care to lie in wait in the forest night after night. At the place in the Grüt where the Züriheiri continued cutting his brushwood, however, they heard the constant crackling and breaking of branches. It was therefore very convenient for them to invent—and themselves believe—the tale that a spirit lived there in the Grüt. When the wood thief passed them on a hidden nighttime path, panting and gasping beneath his load of Reiswellen, the sluggish men could not see him, of course, but the more distinctly they heard him breathe, the more widely the ghost story spread. Then one night the mayor, the son of the old proprietor of the Ox Inn, had to walk home late from the village of Tegerfelden to Zurzach, and his path led through the Grüt. The wood thief was once again at work in the bright moonlight, cutting and binding his Reiswellen. But his panting and coughing and the snapping and crackling of the branches did not hold the mayor back. He stepped into the thicket and recognized his friend at first glance. “Just wait, Züriheiri,” he said indignantly. “The council will teach you!” With that, he meant to continue on his way. But the man who had been caught blindly seized his Hag-Gertel, ran after the mayor, and drove its curved point into the back of his neck. The stricken man could say only, “Oh, what an evil thing you have done! I would not have reported you!” Then he died. At this, all love suddenly awoke again in the murderer. He threw the bloody Gertel far away, flung himself down howling, rolled upon the earth, and wanted to despair. But then fear came over him. He dragged the body into the bushes, buried it beneath the leaves, and fled. By roundabout ways he reached his house while it was still night and thereafter kept himself shut inside. The body was soon found, however, together with the bloody Gertel nearby, and everyone immediately recognized it as the Züriheiri's. All the oaths the accused offered to swear before the Blutgericht were now in vain. The judge drew a black cloth from the table and ordered him to place his three Schwörfinger into the corpse's wound, for the body had suddenly been uncovered there. With trembling knees the accused attempted it. A stream of blood sprang from the corpse into his face and covered him until he was pulled away from the murdered man. “The All-Knowing One has judged!” cried the judges, and the convicted man said, “Yes, he has.” On the same spot in the Grüt where the deed occurred, he was then put to death and buried.
