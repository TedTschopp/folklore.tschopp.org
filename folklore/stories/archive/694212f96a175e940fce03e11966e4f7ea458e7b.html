At first the old episcopal city would not have been unwilling to become Swiss; but when the impetuous Confederates once attacked it and tried to force it by violence into confederate affection, the enraged people of Constance renounced their friendship and joined the Swabian League. This and other causes brought about the outbreak of the so-called Swabian War. The two sides fought back and forth and inflicted much loss and damage on one another, although the Swabians prudently remained behind the Rhine and Lake Constance. The Swiss now hastily set out with all their Panner to meet the emperor’s army, for if his forces triumphed, dreadful times awaited their country. Assault after assault was repelled by the brave Tyrolean marksmen positioned behind it. When the Austrian enemy also came storming against their rear in bright ranks, the Swiss had to fear being crushed. Then their leader Fontana swept them into one last furious attack on the earthwork, and they succeeded in taking the heavily defended wall. Fontana was the first to stand beyond it. But then a sword thrust struck him in the abdomen. His comrades in arms faltered as if paralyzed for a moment when they saw their commander sink to his knees. Yet he rose once more, stretched his sword high, and called out, pressing the dreadful wound in his belly closed with one hand: “Confederates, boldly at them! Do not concern yourselves with my fall.
