She preferred to listen to the enticements of the wealthy knight of Rätenberg, to turn her back upon her parental home and her mother's admonitions and tears, and to move to Rätenberg to spend her life in frivolity and shame. The knight of Rätenberg had driven his lawful wife from the castle; now he bound Nesa to himself with rich gifts and with every pleasure that time and place could offer, and Nesa allowed herself to be dazzled by the splendor. She did not shrink from hunting in the Fetzwaldung around Brunberg upon a finely harnessed horse and in magnificent clothing, galloping past the windows of her parents' home while sounding the horns. This was too much for her mother's feelings; grief over her daughter's errors cast her upon her deathbed. Sensing that death was near, the mother wished to make one final attempt to reform her daughter. For years, all contact between mother and daughter had ceased, but now she sent a servant asking her daughter to come for the final hour of her life and hear her last word. At another time, her husband and sons would have opposed such a message, but now they allowed it. After leaving the house, the servant heard the noise of the hunt in the Fesswald. He had scarcely entered the forest's darkness when a stag rushed past him. A pack of dogs soon followed, and behind them came the knight of Rätenberg and Nesa on horseback.\n‘For God's sake, mistress, stop!’ cried the messenger. ‘Your mother lies dying and wishes to see you once more. Come, and do not delay even a moment.’ Nesa had indeed halted her horse and looked uncertainly at the knight. ‘Pah!’ he cried. ‘Old women intend to die every day and yet never do. If she dies, she will surely find Heaven; but we shall lose the stag. Forward, forward!’ He spurred his horse, struck the other as well, and the hunt thundered onward. When the servant brought this report back to Brunberg, the most terrible fury rose in the hearts of the father and brothers. The mother let a tear fall from her eye, folded her hands, and spoke a quiet prayer. Everyone felt that something was happening here that would have been impossible for them: the mother was praying for her lost daughter. Suddenly, however, the supplicant's lips twitched; the angel of death had received her prayer and her soul.", "Matters continued in this way for a few more years. Then poverty entered Ratenberg, and at last the knight himself had to sell his castle. He joined an expedition to Jerusalem as a common soldier, and no one ever heard of him again. Nesa, meanwhile, became an itinerant singer. The songs once performed for her at sumptuous meals in Rätenberg she now sang herself, in shabby clothing, in the courtyards and halls of other castles and even in marketplaces, earning a meager livelihood. Suddenly, one of the devastating diseases that were so frequent and deadly in those times seized her, and with the sickness came a powerful longing to see her father's house once more. Carrying death within her, she dragged herself toward home and reached the very place at the entrance to the Fesswald where the road from Rickenbach to Kirchberg divides into two branches, but she could go no farther. Like a ghost, a punishing memory rose from the ground: this was the place where her mother's servant had called to her and summoned her to the deathbed. Beneath the first fir tree to the right of the footpath toward Kirchberg, Nesa collapsed and gave up her spirit. Yet a higher power denied that spirit the paths into the world beyond. A dying mother's tear closed Heaven to her; the dying mother's prayer closed the place of punishment and purification to her. Thus the soul remained bound to the place where it had left the body. As soon as twilight passed into night, travelers saw a sinister-looking female being sitting beneath the fir. Age—or sin and hardship—had furrowed the face deeply, and the lurking fire of the eyes involuntarily made every heart contract. This female figure was the departed Nesa, that poor soul who had chosen her path in life so badly. Yet Nesa's straying soul retained its evil inclinations. Whenever a traveler unfamiliar with the way asked the little woman beneath the fir for the right path to Wolfikon and Kirchberg, a lightning-like gleam shot from her eyes and she pointed out a path. Woe to anyone who followed her direction. For hours, the traveler wandered through the woods and, in stormy weather, contracted illness in summer and found death in winter. Nearby residents therefore soon understood the true nature of the female being beneath the fir. They warned their children and household members against passing the haunted place in darkness; when the Fessfräulein sat beneath the fir, they were not to ask her any question or listen to her, but piously make the sign of the cross and hurry past.", "A pious hand familiar with Nesa's story did erect an image of the Mother of God upon the fir beneath which the Fessfräulein had her seat, hoping thereby to break the power of that final tear shed by Nesa's mother and open the daughter's path into the world beyond. In vain! For centuries more, the Fessfräulein continued her harmful activity. People still live who claim to have seen her apparition. Then, on a stormy winter evening, an 18-year-old girl came hurrying along the footpath. Fear and sorrow showed in her features, and her step was urgent. Suddenly, at the entrance to the Fesswald, she stopped; she did not know which path to choose. She looked around and, behold, beneath the first fir sat an old little woman who nodded to her. ‘For God's sake,’ cried the girl, ‘which way does one go to Wolfikon?’ The little woman gazed at the girl for a time. Nesa had been of precisely this age and adorned with the same bloom of youth when she entered the path of transgression. It seemed as though Nesa's spirit were being shown an image of her former self. At last, however, the uncanny fire flashed from the little woman's eye. Silently, she raised her arm and pointed out the path—it was the wrong one. The girl was about to rush that way and thus to her destruction. Then she looked once more at the little woman and saw the mocking smile upon her lips. ‘For God's merciful sake,’ cried the girl, ‘if you yourself are the Fessfräulein, have mercy and show me the right path. My mother lies upon her deathbed and wishes to give me her blessing one last time! Oh, do not deprive me of my salvation; I have nothing else. Do not deprive my mother of the final sight of her daughter! Human being or spirit, God will reward you!’ Speaking thus and wringing her hands, she knelt before the little woman. The little woman remained silent for a long time. At last, a ray of light broke from her eye, but it was entirely different from before: a gentler, reassuring light. She raised her arm and showed the supplicant another path, the right one, and her face became so gentle that the girl hurried away without the slightest doubt. Now the hour had also come that purified and freed Nesa's soul. Through this compassion for the fate of a girl hurrying to her dying mother, she had atoned for her own transgression, and her spirit rose into other realms. The image of Mary still stands today upon a fir at the entrance to the Fesswald, and travelers still hurry warily past that tree; nevertheless, no human eye ever saw the Fessfräulein again.\nC. G. J.
