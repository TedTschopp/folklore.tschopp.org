Beforehand, the executioner cut the rooster open and found three more eggs inside it. This was done pursuant to a judicial sentence by the Freiknaben and in the presence of a great crowd from town and country, who were not a little terrified by the rare occurrence. It had always been held that from such a rooster's egg—if the rooster is seven years old and the egg is incubated in dung by a snake called Coluber, though others believe the sun's heat alone can do it—there emerges a basilisk, an animal half rooster and half snake. Though no more than a few handspans long, it is more fearful and terrible than the greatest Lindwurm or dragon, for its mere gaze kills. This will surely seem less wondrous to anyone who knows that the ray of its eyes is so sharp that it shatters even the hardest stone. The basilisk possesses this terrible property only in sunlight; therefore, people who once had such an animal in a cellar had to keep it closed throughout the year so that no ray of sunlight entered. It is also told that brush or grass over which the basilisk passes—for it does not crawl like a snake but walks upright—instantly withers and is deprived of all vital force. As the most dreadful example of the poison dwelling within it, people say that if a mounted man pierces such an animal with his lance, the poison darting up the lance kills horse and rider on the spot. Only one means exists to render such a basilisk harmless, that is, to kill it: one holds a mirror before it so that it sees itself. When this happens, it immediately falls over dead. Finally, what is told of the city of Basel—that it took its name from the finding of such an animal—is erroneous, although the finding itself is true, according to an old chronicle.
