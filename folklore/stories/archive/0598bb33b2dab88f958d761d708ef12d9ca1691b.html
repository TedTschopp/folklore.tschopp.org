The Dorfwüör ran through the kitchen. And because there was no window in the kitchen, one had almost no light upon entering, even in broad daylight. In the darkness, one had to pass beside the Dorfwüör, which rushed so eerily, and had to grope around with one’s hands to find the parlor door. If one finally found and opened it, one entered a black, half-dark, oppressive room that made one begin to fear even during the day, especially when one remembered what old people had told about this little house—and how uncanny and full of Boze it was said to be at night. People say that at night the sounds of piste und towwu were often heard in the parlor; something without a head often sat behind the stove. If anyone went out of the parlor or into it at night, a headless male figure followed and finally disappeared behind the stove. Often something was heard rolling out from behind the stove, and when people looked, it was now an ugly dead person’s head, now what looked like the freshly severed little head of a girl, and now the little head of a young boy. At times it sounded as if someone slammed shut a large chest—and afterward one heard meat being chopped on a block; at other times there came pitiful lamenting in sudden bursts—and afterward a voice growing softer and softer, as though someone were murdering a young woman. People often saw what looked like a black heap of money on the floor, and when they struck it with a stick, it scattered like a swarm of small blowflies. But it would take too long to recount all these tales of the Boze; that is enough of them. The reason it was so uncanny in this little house came from a gruesome murderer who is said to have lived here in the most ancient times. This murderer was named Jani, a foreigner who had married a local woman. He had a half-grown stepdaughter living with him, toward whom he was harshly disposed. The mother was always afraid for the poor child because she knew what sort of man he was. Then once a little beggar boy came into the house and asked for alms. Jani said to him: \"Yes, I will give you alms so that you will no longer be hungry today—come with me; I will give you pretty red apples.\" Then the boy went upstairs with him. There Jani lifted a large, heavy chest lid and said to him: \"Look there, what beautiful red apples those are; take whatever you want.\" When the boy tried to reach down into the chest, Jani struck him on the head with the chest lid and strangled the poor child in this way. Afterward, on a chopping block, he cut off the boy’s head, hacked the body to pieces, and threw it into the Dorfwüör. He carried the child’s head far from the house and placed it in the fork of a tree. But the poor stepdaughter fared no better. Once, when the mother was ill, he ordered the girl to go with him into the Eye to gather wood. \"Oh, leave her here with me,\" the mother pleaded and begged. \"Who is to look after me in my illness?\" \"Oh, she will soon be back,\" said the murderer, \"but this time she must come with me.\" \"Ah,\" the mother said, weeping, \"then may God protect you! I shall never see you again!\" When the murderer had gone some distance into the Eye, he ran after the girl with an open knife. The poor girl would still have escaped him if her beautiful long braids had not caught in a bush. Thus the murderer caught and killed her, cut the flesh from her body piece by piece, and threw it into the Rottu. People watched him from across the Rottu and shouted, but he acted as if he did not hear them until he had completed his bloody work. But now the murderer’s time was up: he was quickly reported to the authorities, arrested, and executed as he deserved.
