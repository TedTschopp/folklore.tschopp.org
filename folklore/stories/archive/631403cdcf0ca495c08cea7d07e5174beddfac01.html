In the middle of the vat is a somewhat suspicious bung-hole—one might say, well suited to secretly drawing off wine. The writer of this legend saw both the vat with the side opening and the round stone beside the cellar post on which the Gewaltshaber customarily stands when he wishes to preside over the municipal council or address it.", "When, at Christmas or on the ‘Grossen Vergeb’—Corpus Christi Day—the great assembly sits comfortably over its cups in the communal building for the traditional drink, the officers also hold their meetings down below in the communal cellar. Naturally these can only be opened after a couple of B’scheide, wooden cupfuls—the first go down quickly—have been poured. When the tones of speech in the assembly room approach the upper octave and the strings threaten to snap altogether, the Gewaltshaber in the cellar understands the state of affairs. He adjourns the meeting, puts a full large double jug, a Marjosi, into each council member’s hand, takes one himself, and with this opens the procession into the communal building. At the sight of the jugs the tone immediately falls by a third or a fifth and soon modulates back into its old familiar humming.", "The stone slab by the cellar post mentioned above receives the reverence due to it. Apart from the Gewaltshaber, certainly no other human being, however gray his hair as citizen or officer may have grown, ever dares set foot upon it. If the uninitiated enter the communal cellar and venture onto it, they are immediately ordered off. They had so much respect for me that they did not warn me of the great venerability of the Gewaltshaber’s stone until I had bent and turned about on it to my satisfaction and happened to leave the slab again. May God rest those good people’s souls!", "But let us return to the vat, which according to the legend takes its name from an old Gewaltshaber named Müller. When he wanted wine for communal purposes without much commotion—that is, without pulling the large Ponten, or bung—he used the side opening described above. The Gewaltshaber was otherwise a man of proven honesty and held office for many years, yet once something human happened to him as well. After he had given the municipality a great drinking feast with cheese and bread, poured the same B’scheide for all the members of the community, and distributed bread and cheese in the prescribed measure and weight, one double jug of wine and two loaves remained. He thought that was surely too little to divide equally among everyone, so he took it home to his family, since he received no other reward for all his labor.", "Years later the well-deserving Gewaltshaber died, but in eternity his soul did not find the rest it desired because of the double jug of wine and the two loaves. His spirit watched for a long time for an opportunity to warn his heirs to make restitution for what he had appropriated for himself. Then one evening as night was falling, a man at the mills by the stream drew irrigation water and intended to guide it, walking along the edge of the watercourse, out into the fields. He had scarcely passed the mills when he noticed below on the road a stranger hurrying ahead of him. Where the road and watercourse crossed, the stranger stood still and called upon the slowly approaching water tender to wait and hear him out. Somewhat alarmed by the stranger’s uncanny nature, the man refused because he had to follow the water lest it cause damage in the nearby fields. He did not stop but hurried onward with the water. The stranger too sadly continued along his road and, passing the bakehouse, entered the village. Below the village of Törbel, in the Hofmatten, the road and watercourse cross again, and the stranger stood there too. He now spoke threateningly to the man approaching slowly: ‘Here in the meadows your water can do no more damage. If you will not hear me here either, I will do to you as I did to Father Tammatter.’ This priest was said to have refused to hear the stranger, claiming that as priest he was master in his own parish and would accept errands or commands from no one. Deeply grieved, the rejected man departed, but made it clear that at the next opportunity he would meet the priest outside the bounds of his parish. And indeed this priest died in the rectory at Visp, where he intended to spend the night so that on ‘Kreuzmittwoche’ he could accompany his parishioners in procession to Glis. He went to bed healthy in the evening and was found dead in the morning.
