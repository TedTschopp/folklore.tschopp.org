He is said to have appropriated considerable sums while serving as guardian to a widow and then to have sworn away the charges when the accounts were settled; at his death, however, he instructed his sons to repay what had been wrongfully acquired. All the sons but one were prepared to carry out their father’s last wish. That one persuaded them that it would be wiser to let the whole affair, and with it the memory of their father, rest once and for all. So it was done, and soon no one thought any more of the dead man. A long time afterward, a maidservant from elsewhere was weeding on an estate near that family. Once, when she straightened up from the field to rest, she saw the dead teacher standing before her. Frightened nearly to death, she ran into the house and told her employers. They laughed at her heartily. Instead of being reassured, however, the maid told her experience to other, more credulous people, and at last it came to the ears of the teacher’s family. They did not hesitate long and summoned the imprudent storyteller before the court. Naturally she could not provide the demanded proof and was sentenced to a fine very large for someone in her circumstances. She paid, but afterward stubbornly maintained her claim. She assured her own employers that she still saw the same figure, whom she could describe accurately and convincingly, and that she could show it in broad daylight near that garden to anyone who wished. Those who took her up on it as a test saw nothing at all; this was explained by saying that they were not Sonntagskinder and therefore did not possess the maid’s second sight. This continued until the following winter. Then, at four o’clock one morning, a thresher went down the road to begin his day labor and noticed, at the place already made known by the court case, a man standing quietly in a cloak and hat. The thresher thought at first that a half-frozen man stood before him; otherwise he could not explain such motionless standing in that winter cold and solitude. He approached and looked up beneath the hat into the man’s face, and with horror recognized the notorious teacher. He ran for safety to the nearest house and collapsed unconscious in its sitting room. When he came to, he explained the cause of his fright. The people urged him to report his encounter at once to the dead man’s sons, for in court they had declared that they would be more inclined to believe the event if a man personally vouched for it, and that in that case they would certainly reimburse the poor maid for the sum she had been made to forfeit on their account. The thresher followed this advice and went to the sons. But instead of repaying the girl, they immediately had two Capuchin friars come and bind the spirit into a roof rafter of the house. Since then, no one has seen anything more there." ], "notes": [ "Swiss term “Sonntagskinder”: literally “Sunday children,” people born on a Sunday who in Swiss and wider German-speaking folklore were credited with unusual luck or second sight; here the term explains why the maid alone can see the ghost, although the precise local belief is not specified." ] }, "legacy_urls": [] } ---
