Many a hunter has experienced that the fox is truly crafty and treacherous. By day, it is shot only when taken by surprise. If a hunter begins following its tracks in fresh snow, the fox plays its merry tricks. It hurries ahead at a measured pace, remaining just beyond shooting range. If it gains too great a lead, it sits down comfortably, braces itself on its forepaws, and looks mockingly back at the slow hunter to see whether he will soon follow. This goes on for a long time, back and forth and up and down. In the end, without fail, the hunter has been led back to the very place he left at the beginning and may start the circuit once more if he wishes.", "In some places, however, it is customary to lie in wait for foxes at night. People lure them with offered food—a Fuchsbeize—to suitable places so that they can shoot from concealed firing holes. They also set traps for them, though these must contain no iron. But it is precisely at night that the fox displays its great craftiness; it seems made and trained for nocturnal roguery. It knows very cleverly how to scent every danger. On its nighttime wanderings, it likes to use many of the people's paths and footbridges. When it meets an unarmed person—and it can tell reliably—it gives scarcely more room than a stable hand in heavy shoes is accustomed to allow on the sidewalk for a fine lady whose mighty crinoline sweeps the street. It regularly snatches away the offered food, scraps of meat—people are glad when the fox grows accustomed to it—whenever the hunter is not at his watch; but if he is peering from the lookout hole, it does not come. The fox is deceived by having the hunter conveyed to the watch on a sled; when it notices that everyone who approached has also gone away again, it believes itself safe.", "During such a night hunt, the good hunter—as one can readily imagine—falls prey to many deceptions that give fresh nourishment to his belief in witches and magic. Thus, for example, it is told that a fox regularly appeared to a man who lay in wait for it at night but played such peculiar games and tricks upon him that he could never take a shot. Growing impatient, he began to suspect witchcraft. He therefore had his powder blessed by the Jesuits in Brig and resolved henceforth to fire without further hesitation. He did so, and the teasing fox immediately vanished from his sight. When day broke, traces of blood showed that his shot had struck. He followed them along the ordinary roads down the mountain into the valley, across the Rhône, and up the mountain again to a house occupied by a woman suspected of witchcraft. He happened to meet a child in front of the house. Asked whether its mother was at home, the child replied that she had just returned home ill; she had developed stabbing pains in her side and was bleeding heavily from one foot. Astonished but satisfied to have deceived and caught the teasing fox at last, our hunter returned home.
