When the farmer came out with the threshing flail, the rider ordered him immediately to take his horse by the bridle and accompany him to Frick. The frightened farmer obeyed. In the twilight, the two reached the broad hollow below Gipf called the Höle—that is, a sunken road—because a cart road formerly passed through it. Even then, the Hölenthier became dangerous here if one tried to pass before Morgenläuten. The farmer therefore released the horse's bridle and stepped back to see how the rider would cross the ill-reputed place. But the rider suspected treachery, and the two began to quarrel. When the Swede threatened him and reached for his weapon, the farmer finally struck him dead with the threshing flail. Ever since, the dead man must haunt this place as a dog whose eyes are like plow wheels. It lies motionless across the road so people will stumble. If struck with a stick, it inflicts a swollen head. Its route runs from Hegel's house opposite the chapel to the footpath behind it, and Hegel's daughter, a Fraufastenkind, often saw it from the window. When our tailor traveled home at night from the Kornberg in this direction, even his yardstick was pulled from under his arm. The spirit also appears as a gaunt, tall man with a broad-brimmed wool hat on his head. With a violent noise of wind, he rushes toward people and takes the hat from their heads. From the Helgenstöckli, a wayside cross, he climbs onto their backs and has himself carried as far as the village. A farmer from Frick who had looked too deeply into his wineglass challenged the spirit on his way home. It appeared in the form of a clergyman in a long black coat, with clerical tabs around his neck and a tricorn on his head, as the local priests dressed in the previous century.
