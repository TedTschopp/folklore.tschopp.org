On the way home toward evening, a dreadful storm overtook him, so that he took shelter beside a solitary barn and, because the torrential rain continued unabated, decided to spend the night there on the hay. Deep in the night, a noise woke him. There before him, he saw a merry and distinguished company. It was—God ‘b'hüt' uns davor’—the Evil One with a number of witches. After the banquet, they came one after another to have their infernal master resolve their questions and difficulties. Now a questioner appeared whom the servant, still unnoticed, recognized very well: it was his master's wife. Understandably, his ears now pricked up, and he heard her complain that her husband had designated one of his most fertile fields for growing grain. The witch, however, was an enemy of bread. She therefore asked what she must do to make the field produce nothing but stalks and empty chaff. And the Devil answered that when the manure was carted onto that field, she should take the form of a toad, ride there in the dung at the back of the wagon, and allow herself to be unloaded. If she managed that, all the ears of grain would be empty. The woman departed happily, but the servant too remembered this. Early in the morning, he hastily left the uncanny place and hurried home, but kept silent about everything he had seen and heard. When it was time to spread manure, he contrived to have the task assigned to him. His sharp eye did not miss the clod and the place at the back of the wagon in which the toad was hiding. During the journey, he seized his Karst, drew it back, and thrust it vigorously again and again into the place in question. He had struck well, for his master soon came running up, panting, and anxiously ordered the faithful servant to hurry to the doctor without delay, as his wife was dying. Now the servant revealed the secret and invited the farmer to investigate.
