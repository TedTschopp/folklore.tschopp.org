Volken died in Bellwald and appeared four times to his son-in-law Fabian Ritz. The first time, Ritz saw him on the Voralpe walking up through the pastures; the second time, at the farmstead, sitting on the straw bench in the barn as though he wished to tend the livestock. Out of fear he could not speak to him, although it was already broad daylight. Once, however, on Christmas Eve, Ritz said to his wife, ‘I shall sleep now. If you stay awake, wake me at eleven o’clock so that I can go to the mill’—which was a quarter of an hour from the village, at a place called ‘am Ried’—‘to see whether the mill is running properly and to put on more grain.’ When he awoke at eleven o’clock, he rose, took his torch, and went toward the mill. As he drew near, he could no longer hear the mill clattering, so he walked faster and, upon entering, looked first of all into the flour bin. But when he turned around, his father-in-law was again standing behind him, accompanied by two companions: a young man dressed in white and a black figure that held the dead man fast with iron chains secured around his body. In fear the miller cried, ‘For God’s sake, what do you lack?’ ‘I lack three things. In Grun I bought a Lagel of wine and did not pay for it. I kept a Federtaler belonging to the church that I had collected without anyone else knowing and believed that this did not burden my conscience. Then there is a journey to Einsiedeln. You must make these three things good and discharge them for me.’ The miller said, ‘I shall restore the Federtaler, and I would gladly pay for the Lagel of wine as well, but I do not know whom to pay. I cannot go to Einsiedeln, for I am too old, but I shall pay someone and send that person for you.’ The dead man answered, ‘The man from whom I bought the wine is dead, but his sons are still alive, and you can give them the money. But you must go to Einsiedeln yourself. I shall help you so that you can walk there.’ When the miller promised to fulfill all three things, the dead man’s companion let the chains fall and rushed terrifyingly down the mountain. The dead man said to him once more, ‘Now I am released. Farewell! In a short time we shall see each other again,’ and vanished.", "In the same year, during the Fasten-Temperwoche, the miller once went to ‘Nesselschluchten’ in the evening to feed the livestock. Because he was still at the stable rather late, the Angelus rang at the stone house. He prayed beside the stable and went home. After a while he saw a beautiful brightness that shone like the clear sun, and the procession of the dead met him. The first figure carried a white cross that spread this radiance; after him they came two by two, side by side, and the procession lasted about a quarter of an hour. His father-in-law came fairly near the end. The miller said to him, ‘What? Are you still coming along here? I thought you had already been released.’ The dead man answered, ‘I am released, but I must still wander. I have neither joy nor sorrow, yet heavenly joy has not yet been granted to me. Soon, however, I shall enjoy that as well.’ Then he also told him, ‘You will see several more people come here whom you knew, and before a few years have passed, you too will walk this path.’ And so it happened. The miller recognized several people in the procession of the dead who had died in Bellwald shortly before, and he himself died soon afterward. The Totengang is said to have been divided into four sections, and with each division there was a priest in a little surplice, with a biretta and a reliquary arm. This was told to me by Joh. Jos.
