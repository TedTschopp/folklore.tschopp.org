In the middle of the night, when the Griffin was snoring very loudly, Hans reached up and tore a feather from his tail. The Griffin suddenly started awake and said, ‘Wife, I smell a Christian, and it seems to me that someone has tugged at my tail.’ The woman said, ‘You must have been dreaming. I already told you today that a Christian was here, but he left again. He told me all sorts of things. At one castle, they have lost the key to a money chest and can no longer find it.’ ‘Oh, the fools,’ said the Griffin. ‘The key is lying in the woodshed behind the door, beneath the woodpile.’ ‘And then he also said that at one castle a daughter was sick, and they knew no remedy to make her well.’ ‘Oh, the fools,’ said the Griffin. ‘Under the cellar stairs, a toad has made a nest from her hair; if she gets the hair back, she will become well.’ ‘And then he also said there was a body of water somewhere and a man beside it who had to carry everyone across.’ ‘Oh, the fool,’ said the Griffin. ‘If only he were to set someone down in the middle just once, he would never have to carry anyone across again.’ Early in the morning, the Griffin rose and went away. Hans came out from beneath the bed with a beautiful feather. He had also heard what the Griffin said about the key, the daughter, and the man. The Griffin’s wife told him everything once more [322] so that he would forget nothing, and then he started homeward. First, he came to the man by the water, who at once asked what the Griffin had said. Hans replied that the man should first carry him across, and then he would tell him on the other side. The man carried him across. Once Hans was over, he told the man that he need only set someone down in the middle just once, and then he would never have to carry anyone across again. The man was immensely pleased and told Hans that, in thanks, he would carry him back and forth once more. Hans said no, he would spare him the trouble; otherwise he was satisfied with him, and he went on. Then he came to the castle where the daughter had been ill. He took her on his shoulder, since she could not walk, carried her down the cellar stairs, brought out the toad’s nest from beneath the lowest step, and placed it in the daughter’s hands. She sprang down from his shoulder and ran up the stairs ahead of him, completely well. Now her father and mother were tremendously happy and gave Hans gifts of gold and silver; whatever he wanted, they gave him. When Hans came to the other castle, he went straight into the woodshed. Behind the door, beneath the woodpile, he duly found the key and brought it to the lord. The lord too rejoiced greatly and, as a reward, gave Hans much of the gold that was in the chest, as well as all sorts of other things, including cows, sheep, and goats. When Hans came to the king with all these things—with the money, the gold and silver, and the cows, sheep, and goats—the king asked where he had obtained it all. Hans said the Griffin gave a person as much as he wanted. The king thought he could use that too and also set off for the Griffin. But when he reached the water, he happened to be the first person to arrive since Hans. The man set him down in the middle and went away, and the king drowned.
