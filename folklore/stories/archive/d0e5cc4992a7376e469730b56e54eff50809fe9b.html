All these treasures are loaded on a great four-wheeled wagon whose rattling and creaking can be heard quite clearly at certain times. But whoever wishes to possess the treasure must appear at that mountain at midnight on Easter Day with horse and ox, for at that time, the wagon's drawbar moves out from the wall of the hill. Whoever can harness the animals and pull the wagon even a short distance outward before an hour has passed owns it from that instant together with all the treasures upon it. But if even a single word is spoken in the process, the wagon rushes clattering back into the mountain, and much further misfortune and harm threaten the careless chatterer.", "The prospect of gaining great wealth at a single stroke, despite the danger attached to it, once tempted a farmer to undertake the venture. In addition to the two horses he already owned, he acquired two other young, spirited animals and four powerful oxen, and he provided himself with new, strong, sturdy harness straps, chains, and ropes. Thus equipped, in the company of a Geisterbanner, he went to the hill on the following Easter night, where according to legend, the wagon with the treasure was supposed to lie. Once there, he could scarcely await the approach of midnight. At last, twelve o'clock struck. A crack from the hill's slope followed the final stroke of the bell, and a drawbar three handspans thick, its tip glowing like sulfurous fire, shot from the earth with the speed of an arrow between the farmer and the Geisterbanner. Although frightened, the men rushed toward it. The animals were quickly harnessed and, urged by powerful blows of the whip, pulled with steam rising from them. The load could not withstand their united strength and began to move. A jerk and another jerk; the earth gaped wider and wider; already, the wagon's front wheels could be seen; already, the golden gleam of the riches piled upon it; already, the rear wheels moved forward, and the treasure hunters now put their hands to the spokes to help—when the farmer, breaking the command of silence, cried triumphantly: ‘Hoho, now we nearly have it!’ Scarcely had this been said when a thunderous crash followed. Seized by invisible spirit hands, the rash chatterer was hurled far away so that he fell unconscious to the ground, while the wagon, together with the Geisterbanner, horses, and oxen, rushed amid dreadful rattling back into the hill, which instantly closed behind it. Only the next morning did the farmer awaken from his unconsciousness. The loss of the animals, and probably also the anxiety and terror he had endured, affected him so badly that he later became mentally ill and ended his life by suicide." ], "notes": [ "Swiss term “Geisterbanner”: literally a ‘spirit binder,’ a ritual specialist believed able to command, banish, or constrain spirits; he accompanies the farmer to manage the supernatural danger, though the broken silence draws him into the mountain too." ] }, "legacy_urls": [] } ---
