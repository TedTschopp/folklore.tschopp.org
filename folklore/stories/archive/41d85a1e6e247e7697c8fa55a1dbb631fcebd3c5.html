That this castle, together with the island upon which it stood, sank into the depths of the lake was the curse of an evil deed committed by the barons of Güttingen, whose ancestral seat was Moosburg Castle. The legend is dreadful:", "At the time when the barons of Güttingen still ruled that region, a great famine came over the land. Every available foodstuff had been consumed. Only in the castle of the barons of Güttingen, whose granaries were amply filled, did people continue mercilessly living in luxury amid the misery of others. At last, when the distress could no longer be borne, the people gathered in crowds and begged the rich lords for a few handfuls of grain to sustain their wretched lives, but every plea of the poor was in vain. Enraged, they gathered their last strength and meant to take by force what their petitions had been denied. Yet what could the exhausted, hunger-weakened people do against the well-fed servants of the barons of Güttingen? They soon had to yield to force, and a great portion of them were captured and brought into Moosburg Castle. The merciless lords, still more enraged by the unfortunate people’s final desperate act, now gave the order to lock the prisoners in a barn and set it on fire. As the flames closed over the poor people and their cries of misery became increasingly stifled, the hardhearted barons called with devilish mockery, “Ah, the Kornmäuslein—the little grain mice—will soon have finished squeaking!” Scarcely had these words crossed their lips when countless black points began swarming inside the fire. The glow grew blacker and blacker, until thousands upon thousands of rats and mice burst from it and furiously attacked the inhuman lords with sharp teeth and claws. The lords fled to their Wasserburg on an island in the lake, hoping to escape God’s judgment there. But their tormentors pursued them even there, and nothing could save them from the dreadful fate of being eaten alive. From then on the castle and island were the dwelling place of those animals. In a short time they were gnawed so thoroughly that soon after their lords’ death they sank into the lake." ], "notes": [ "Swiss term “Kornmäuslein”: literally ‘little grain mice,’ this is the barons’ cruel nickname for starving petitioners, answered by the supernatural arrival of real rodents; whether the wording derives from an older local formula is uncertain." ] }, "legacy_urls": [] } ---
