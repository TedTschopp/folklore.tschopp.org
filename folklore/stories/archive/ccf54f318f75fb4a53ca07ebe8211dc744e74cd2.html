They wore black-gray outer shirts reaching from their shoulders to the ground, a black belt around the body, wooden boards instead of shoes, and always went bareheaded.", "Their visits were to a humane and cheerful master of the house who lived in continual harmony with all his family. Early in the morning, they appeared at his home, went into the fields with him, and helped with the work; only at mealtimes did they stay away, accepting nothing that might be set before them. In the morning, they were always particularly merry and cheerful, but by evening, they grew visibly quieter, and when the prayer bell sounded in the neighboring village of Leuggern, they hurried away lamenting. They all ran to a particular place on the bank of the Aare and plunged into the water; at that same spot, however, they emerged again the following morning and went to their dear farmer. When winter came and there was no more fieldwork, they entertained the family with all kinds of feats. Thus, in the barn, where they liked to be best in any case, they burned four-sided, six-sided, and even perfectly circular holes through the middle of a bundle of straw, as one requested, without another stalk beside it catching fire. For they knew how to bind fire. Because of these arts, they were also called Heidenzwerge; but whether they truly were Heiden, no one knows to this day.", "As long as they visited the farmer, his prosperity increased, and the insatiable neighbor often envied him for it and wished to draw the dwarfs to himself as well. But this did no good; they remained faithful to the old man, who lived contentedly and happily with the entire household until his death. After he died and his two sons inherited the property, the envious neighbor soon succeeded in setting them at odds. The good-natured dwarfs were all the more grieved to hear quarreling and strife in the formerly peaceful family. Soon, they no longer appeared daily; then their number declined; finally, only one came. But at last, even this one left the two brothers with these words: ‘Our time is now over, and therefore we shall never, perhaps not for eternity, see you again; but we shall remember your kindnesses forever.
