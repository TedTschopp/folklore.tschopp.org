In the mountain forest he shot his Sunday roast, and he netted Lenten trout by the score in the valley brook that winds between the rocky gorge and the sunny slope. One day in high summer, while he was alone at the farm and all his servants were out on the land, a butcher came up from the valley and asked for the sort of fat suckling calves that thrive only on mountain pasture. He was led into the stable, and while he bent over each animal to judge its muzzle, dewlap, and shoulder according to his trade, the Senn noticed the bulging money belt fastened around his body. A vile thought flashed through the landowner’s mind. He seized a milking stool and struck the man down from behind. He buried the body in the dung heap before the stable and locked the money belt with its Brabant thalers in the chest beneath his bed. Not a soul had heard or seen it, and by the time the servants came home that evening, every trace of what had happened had already been erased. A whole year passed, and the butcher remained missing. Summer returned. Once again, the Senn sat home alone, and again every last servant was up on the meadows, mowing vigorously. This time, however, the hay was so abundant that the many small sheds and emergency ricks could not hold it all. Instead of carting it in, some had to be piled on Heinzen and around Tristenstöcke and left outside until buyers came up from the valley. The jubilant calls of the haymakers could be heard all the way down at the house. The Senn alone, to whom everything fell and everything belonged, did not seem able to share their joy. Tired and dull as he was, the path to the nearest lower or upper pasture seemed much too far to him this time. Only now and then did he look through the corner window toward the servants above, watch the day’s work nearing its end, and sit down again on his window bench. Then his gaze fell across toward the stable and the great dung heap, where he saw grass standing high and thick in clumps upon a single patch. Those few lush tufts of grass caught his bone-dry avarice. He rose from behind the bench, took the scythe from its nail outside, and was not ashamed, before the eyes of his servants who were just bringing loads into the yard on hay sledges and wagons, to mow the dozen blades growing around the dung heap. But wait—what was that? The farmer’s scythe struck a stone there with a clang. He himself screamed loudly for help. Everyone ran over, seeking the cause of his terror, and all together saw the same dreadful sight. His scythe had cut into an old human skull over which grass had already grown. A fresh gash ran through the bare bone, and not only the ground but the Senn himself was sprayed all over with blood. Despairing, he rolled about at their feet, struggled between lying and confessing, stammered out half his crime, and within an hour had raved himself out of his senses.
