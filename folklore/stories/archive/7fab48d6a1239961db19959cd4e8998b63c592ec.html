Popular tradition tells of a Count Heinrich who is said to have lived here in the 12th century. He was a gloomy, hot-tempered man. His wife, Countess Ida, however, was a kindhearted woman loved by all the people. One day, she laid her jewelry in the sun at the window. A raven flew over and stole her most beautiful ring, which the count had once given her. At that same hour, a hunter was hunting in the forest thicket. He saw the raven fly in, climbed to its nest, and found the ring there without recognizing it. \nFoolishly, he put it on his own finger as a rightful find and thereby attracted the attention of the castle servants. Soon the rumor spread that the countess was unfaithful to her husband and had given the ring to the young hunter.\nWhen the count heard this, he became beside himself with rage, hurried to his wife's room, and threw her through the open window into the dreadful abyss below. The hunter also protested his innocence in vain; Heinrich had him tied to the tail of the wildest horse and dragged to death.\nA good angel caught the countess in her fall so that she suffered no harm. She found a quiet hiding place in the forest and lived there for many years until at last she was discovered by one of her husband's servants. The count hurried there, heard of his wife's innocence, begged forgiveness on his knees, and invited her to return with him to the castle. The devout woman forgave him but declared that she would no longer leave the forest where God had visibly drawn near to her. She spent the rest of her life in prayer in a little hut. She also attended worship every day in Fischingen, accompanied there by a tame stag. Her stone tomb is still shown today in the monastery church there." ], "notes": [] }, "legacy_urls": [] } ---
