He taught the bright children from the mountains of Spiringen and Unterschächen. But instead of teaching them the Lord's Prayer and initiating them into the mysteries of letters and numbers, he entered with them into the romantic realm of sorcery. That was more entertaining; no wonder the young mountain folk listened to the strange teacher with full attention and made rapid progress. They had already learned to b'stellen people—to bind them in place by magic—and to make living little mice. But they could not give the poor mice legs, so the creatures rolled about helplessly. The ecclesiastical and secular authorities of the municipality and the parents knew nothing of all this, for at their neglectful master's command the little rogues kept their activities and arts secret. One day several of these young sorcerers saw the local priest, Peter Alois Arnold (priest at Spiringen 1805–1831, † 1831), known as the Strassmeister-Heer, coming up near Baldrig. “Wait, we will play a trick on him; we will b'stellen him,” one of them said. The proposal was put into action; the priest suddenly stood still and for several moments could go no farther. But he was no fool and knew more than merely how to eat bread. He simply exchanged the shoes on his feet—that is, he put the right shoe on his left foot and the left shoe on his right foot (according to others, he merely stepped out of his shoes)—and with that the spell was broken. Because he suspected the giggling boys, he conducted a strict investigation, so that the hypocritical Waldbruder's full guilt came to light. The devout people drove him away and from that time onward tolerated no more hermits in Getschwyler.", "Fr. Jos. Arnold and others", "2. Several lads who had outgrown the ordinary school received a kind of further education from the Waldbruder (the fahrender Schüler) in Espen. Still named among them are Rytti-Jaggi-Märti, Herger Lunzi, Fuhrliger Mariä, and Bielen-Maxi's father, all of whom had been personally known to the oldest people of the generation then living. In reality, however, the Waldbruder taught them all manner of magic tricks, and the boys had already become quite accomplished. They could make real, living little mice, though still without legs; they could fly a little; and they could cause the “Streiwipinggel” to come flying through the air from the Schattigen, high above the valley, across to them in the Sonnigen. The teacher could put them completely under his spell. Once Herger Lunzi ran away from him; the teacher calmly called after him, “Linzäli, go on; you will soon come back,” and Linzäli immediately turned around. It was a feast day celebrated by the people and clergy in the chapel. After the morning service, the priest went home but returned after the midday meal for Vespers. Meanwhile, as was then customary, the high-spirited lads amused themselves at skittles.⁴ Time passed quickly, and when the priest became visible below at Baldrig, one of the players said: “Der hätt etz oi bi mym Eich, noch nitt so gleitig miäßä chu!”—approximately, “By my oath, he did not need to come quite so quickly now!” One of the sorcerer's apprentices who was also present made the impudent proposal: “Come on! We'll b'stellen him!” The idea found favor, and one of them bound him. The priest came nonetheless, but he seems to have felt the magic—he must surely have become aware of it—for as he passed the young sinners, he looked at them sternly and raised a warning forefinger. After the service ended, he summoned them, reproved their fault, and spoke to them in a fatherly way. They candidly confessed everything and repented of their wrongdoing; they had not thought they were committing a serious offense. They became so enraged at their seducer that one evening they set a trap before the door of his little house that would inevitably have killed him when he went out.
