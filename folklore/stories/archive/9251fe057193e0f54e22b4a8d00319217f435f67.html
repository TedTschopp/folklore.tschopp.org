Despite his power and standing, however, the earthly judge's punishment overtook him for the many crimes he had committed. He was condemned to death. Proud as he was, he forestalled the execution of this judgment by killing himself. As a suicide, he was then thrown into the Tiber for the fish to devour, as was the custom at that time. But scarcely had this been done when it was as though the gates of hell opened. A storm began to rage and would not end. People then realized that this upheaval of the elements arose from nothing other than Pilate's body, so inappropriately thrown into the Tiber. That river so abhorred his crimes that it would not keep him in its waters. With great effort, therefore, the body was searched for again, and behold: when it was found, the storm subsided. Yet because the corpse had to be placed somewhere, it was taken to Vienne in the Dauphiné and thrown into the Rhône, which gave it the same reception. Amid thunder and lightning, a storm advanced until the inhabitants of that country pulled the uninvited guest from the water and, to rid themselves of him, carried him to Lausanne. But because Pilate caused storm and tempest there too, just as in Italy and Gaul, the people of Lausanne finally resolved, after mature deliberation, to throw him into a small lake in the Alps about forty hours from their city. At last he remained in this dwelling, though not without leaving it from time to time and roaming the Alps as a ghost. He was seen now wading through the mire of his lake, now sitting upon a rock, now in a violent quarrel with another ghost, King Herod, and now racing fleetingly through the mountains. Yet he was always the same evil spirit who covered the surrounding region with storm and tempest, frightened the herders on the pastures, scattered their flocks, and drove the best livestock from rocky cliffs into the abyss. When Pilate began to behave ever worse, and his body nevertheless had to be kept in the lake because no other country would receive it, the people of that region at least wanted to see whether he could be brought to rest. It happened that a fahrender Schüler who had studied at Salamanca and belonged to the Rosenkreuzer had just come to Switzerland. He was promised a large sum of money if he would free the country from the vexations of that evil spirit and bring it to rest forever. The Rosenkreuzer accepted the offer and promised to do everything possible. He immediately set out in pursuit of the spirit and soon encountered it on a high rocky peak. He began his conjurations. These were probably not strong enough; at any rate, Pilate did not move from the spot. The Rosenkreuzer saw himself compelled to prepare stronger formulas of conjuration. For this purpose he went to a hill opposite the rocky peak where Pilate sat. Only then did the real struggle begin, and it became so violent that, even today, part of that hill remains without turf from the conjurer's stamping feet. At last the formulas became so strong that Pilate could no longer resist. He yielded far enough to promise that henceforth he would behave quietly in the lake if he were given a spirit transformed into a black mare, so that he could return to his dwelling in a manner worthy of a Roman knight, and if he were further permitted to rise to the upper world once each year. These conditions were granted. When, at the Rosenkreuzer's command, a black mare actually appeared before Pilate, he swung himself upon it and, enraged by his defeat, spurred the animal into such violent leaps that even today the impression of its hind feet can be seen on one of the rocks around the lake, which since that time has borne the name Pilatussee.", "Pilate has faithfully kept his pact ever since.
