Every territorial lord had the right to pronounce a death sentence for offenses committed within the district under his jurisdiction. Such districts or jurisdictions were called Freigerichte. There must therefore have been many unreliable judgments, several of which still live in popular speech.", "A man in Täsch at zer Metjen named Peter Joseph Schallbetter, who married in 1752 and fathered a daughter in 1754, was accused of having cursed the weather and the authorities. One aggravating circumstance was, to be sure, that he was rich and had many cows in his stable. He was condemned to death for his swearing. A period of grace had to be granted to him, however, because he appealed to the Bishop of Sion, prefect over Valais. The noble chief pastor pardoned him. The judges could have foreseen this, but it did not suit them because of the rich inheritance. They therefore secretly appointed men to delay the messenger in the Kipferwald until the appointed time if necessary, while ordering the condemned man's execution for the exact minute that the law required. When the messenger arrived with all possible haste, he could already see the execution ground crowded with people in the distance. Fearing that he would arrive too late, he waved a white handkerchief high in the air and cried as loudly as he could, “Pardon! Pardon!” But it was too late.
