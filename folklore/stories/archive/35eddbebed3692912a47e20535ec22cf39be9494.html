Just as he looked around to see what the weather might do, a little dwarf in a long shaggy cap stood beside him and asked for a handful of hay. The farmer looked at the little fellow in astonishment, and because he was no taller than an eight-year-old boy, answered with a laugh: ‘You may have as much as you can carry away in one load!’ The dwarf seemed very satisfied with this answer, went into the barn, and soon hay began raining out through the gable. It did not stop until the last forkful lay outside. The little farmer could scarcely recover from his amazement; he did not trust his eyes when he saw the dwarf bind all the hay into a single load and prepare to carry it away. ‘Stop, you rogue!’ the haymaker now cried. ‘That was not what I meant. If you have your way, my cattle and I will fare badly this winter.’ The little dwarf answered: ‘Do not worry. When all your hay is used up, let me know.’ With that, the little fellow disappeared behind the nearest wood. A long winter came over the land, and long before the first", "signs of spring, the hay was consumed down to the last reserve. The farmer scarcely knew what to do. One day, as he paced desperately back and forth in the hayloft, unable to think of any answer to his need, the dwarf suddenly appeared among the beams and said: ‘Entrust your cattle to me. You, however, must go into the stable every day and perform your daily work as though nothing had happened and your animals were standing in their stalls. You must only promise not to utter any curse or blasphemy during the whole time.’ After long consideration, the man agreed to the proposal. Meanwhile, the little dwarf had driven the herd away. The farmer watched the departing company for a long while, but afterward did as he had been told. To be sure, while feeding and milking in the empty stable, he could scarcely wait for spring. One day he grew vexed by the foolish work and began cursing aloud. The words had scarcely escaped his lips when he thought he heard the great bell of his lead cow. He quickly climbed to the gable loft and peered out through the hatch. The bell seemed to come from very near the foot of the Eiger. Then the bells of the entire Sennerei sounded still closer, and now his herd truly appeared at the edge of the nearby wood, round and fat, with a calf beside every cow. The dwarf sat on the rearmost cow. When the procession reached the stable, the little rider jumped to the ground, shook his finger at the farmer, and cried: ‘Had you kept your promise, I would have kept the cattle longer too.’ With that he vanished. But when the Senn examined the last cow, she had only three teats.
