Just as he stepped from the barn with his Melkstuhl strapped on and a bucket in his hand, he saw a little man approaching. It had a long, snow-white beard and an ancient face. It wore a little leather cap upon its head and carried a small bag of livestock salt at its shoulder. Astonished, the farmer stopped on the path. The dwarf greeted him kindly, lifting its cap. Then, in the most urgent tones, it asked the farmer to lend it a cow for the summer. At first the man shook his head, for he did not trust the affair. When the little man would not cease begging, however, he remembered that he still had a skinny little cow, a true Blag that had been wasting away all this time. It would be no loss if he never saw this animal again. He merely wished to remove the bell first, because he was reluctant to lose it. But once more the little man implored him to leave the cow together with the Schelle. “On Michaelmas you shall receive everything back together with the Lehenzins,” it promised. The farmer agreed, and soon he saw the strange dwarf depart with the cow it held on Lehen. The bell sounded ever farther away, and he thought: I shall probably never see either of you again! After both vanished into the mountain forest, he began milking the pressing herd. One morning, when he stepped outside to examine the weather, he saw his little cow high on the mountain, grazing among the firs. Now he believed it completely lost, for where ordinarily only chamois lived, it would surely soon plunge to its death. A few days later he moved farther up to the high Alp with the herd and forgot the whole bargain. Yet matters went remarkably well for him throughout that summer. His cattle thrived, becoming round and glossy, while the neighbors complained of all manner of diseases. Gradually autumn arrived, and the farmer returned to the Vorsäss. On Michaelmas he sat before his hut enjoying the mild sunshine. Then he heard a cowbell ringing with a cheerful sound. Looking around curiously, he saw the little man with the snow-white beard approaching. By the hand it led a cow that gleamed wie die Seide am Zapfen—like silk upon its spool. “Here I bring back the head of cattle you gave me on Lehen,” said the dwarf. “Now tell me how much interest I owe you!” The farmer’s eyes nearly fell from his head in astonishment. He could scarcely believe that this sleek cow with the swollen udder was the skinny Blag he had leased to the little man. At last he recovered and said that he desired no interest at all. “Only come back in spring; I shall gladly let you have another cow for the Sömmerung!” But the little man said that it now had milk and butter in abundance for the rest of its life. Then it handed the farmer a Bratkäslein but warned him never to eat it entirely; then he would always have cheese again. The farmer laughed and shook his head unbelievingly. “Thio und liog!” cried the little man, and that meant: Do as you were told, and you will see! Then it darted away and vanished into its mountain forest. That evening the farmer tasted the strange little cheese and gave some to his wife. Both found that it had an especially fine flavor. They put away what remained, and behold, the next morning it was whole again as before. Now they guarded it like a treasure but without becoming stingy, for they often invited the neighbors to share a meal. For two years they enjoyed what the little man had brought as Lehenzins. But one day the tailor and shoemaker arrived at the same time to work auf der Stör. Without the farmer noticing, the two ate the little cheese down to the last morsel.
