Suddenly, he stood before a mighty sandstone rock that at the time formed an island in the bed of the wild Sense. Upon its summit, as if issuing a challenge and mocking him, stood the object of his pursuit. He quickly spurred his noble horse through the arm of the river and sought a way onto the rock. Soon, he found a game trail by which the animals customarily went to drink. Following it, he began climbing the colossus. He had not yet reached half its height when a mighty Lindwurm rushed at him from a cave. With a powerful arm, however, he thrust his hunting spear into the monster's jaws, then with one tremendous stroke of his heavy sword severed the hideous head from the twitching body. Only then did he climb the rest of the way onto the rock island to search for his true quarry. To his astonishment, the deer itself came toward him, lay down at his feet, and looked up with pleading and at the same time grateful eyes, as if wishing both to thank him for killing its hideous neighbor and to beg him to spare its life. The noble knight understood the animal's silent yet eloquent gaze, was moved, and granted it its life. The deer rose happily, walked ahead of the hunter, and, continually looking back to see whether its rescuer followed, led him all around the rock. The Roman took this as an invitation to possess the rock. Since its romantic setting pleased him exceedingly, he decided to build a hunting castle upon it. Soon afterward, he went to Aventikum and told the commander there of his adventure and decision, adding that the rock was admirably suited to a river fort. The commander immediately gave him workers, and within a short time the rock was crowned with a stately castle. The road intended to connect Helikon with Aventikum was soon routed past this place, and a bridge was built over the Sense beside the castle; on the far bank, the remains of its abutment can still be seen. The castle was called Crassusburg after its founder. A small garrison was assigned to protect the road passing by, and during the fair season Crassus used the fortress as a hunting castle from which he could make hunting excursions into the game-rich surroundings. This legend concerns only the larger western part of the castle. The eastern portion with the tower is plainly of a later date, as its architecture and masonry demonstrate. Tradition says that this part was built by a welschen Raubritter, a Romance-speaking robber knight who, driven from his homeland for his cruelty, fled to this lonely region and wished to found a new robber's nest. At first, he had the old, ruined Roman castle restored by foreign master builders with the help of local laborers and workers. In the beginning, he paid them well and knew how to make himself popular in the region, so people willingly entered his protection. But scarcely had he acquired some rights over the farmers when his brutality and cruelty broke forth. They appeared especially during construction of the second building. The poor country people had to carry the heavy stones for the second structure upon their bleeding shoulders; anyone who showed signs of resisting was immediately struck dead, and his blood was mixed into the masons' mortar.
