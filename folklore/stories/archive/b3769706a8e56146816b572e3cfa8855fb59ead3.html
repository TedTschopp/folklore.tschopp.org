When a majority of the village citizens agreed, the work really began. They started breaking open the ground on the side of the choir where the court officials and municipal elders had their pews. After some shoveling, they struck an unusually large, rough stone slab. It was much too large for a mere gravestone; behind it lay a broad empty space that seemed remarkably clear of rubble and earth, so they dug ahead all the more vigorously. At last, they encountered the blocks of a staircase leading downward. The rejoicing was great but brief, for despite every objection from the diehard believers, they finally had to recognize this stairway as merely a piece of one that everyone had already known about and no one cared about any longer. It had led to the old prisons that the Bernese government had created in the sandstone clefts of this Kirchberg for the rebels of Upper Aargau during the Swiss Peasants' War. These wretched Keichen later fell into decay and were filled in again; a nearby resident also used some of them as an emergency cellar, and so the surviving staircase here still met the cellar wall of the parsonage.", "Now, of course, the golden calf and all the other treasures had to be abandoned. The conclusion of the ridiculous undertaking remained equally peculiar, however. For who was now to bear the costs, refill the churned-up ground, and have the church choir freshly paved? Those original advisers were clever enough to spread all sorts of talk that freed them from paying. Some suddenly remembered again how the Bernese horsemen had once ravaged, burned, and plundered here in the village, and how many poor peasants from Suhr and Entfelden had been hanged from the village linden or thrown into these holes in the mountain. Others, however, claimed that these underground passages did indeed date from the pagan priests who had lived here, and that the parish priests of Suhr had necessarily always been familiar with these and other secret matters of the Kirchberg. In short, by a second municipal resolution, the pastor at that time, Rufli, was ordered to have this passage from the parsonage to beneath the church walled up at his own expense.
