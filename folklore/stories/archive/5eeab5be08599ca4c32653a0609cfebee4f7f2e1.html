The legend of this Bachplätschi or Nachtplätschi is widespread enough in the Surb Valley and extends from the village of Döttingen on the Aare to the Zurich border. Yet people are partly uncertain about its essential meaning and partly no longer concern themselves with it. Pilate, who must wander without rest because of his unjust judgment upon the Savior and usually remains in the regions nearest Mount Pilatus, sometimes revisits his Promised Land and always takes the route through the Surb Valley. Because he is a fiend, however, he can undertake the journey only in an enchanted shape, sometimes as a horse and foal, sometimes as a calf or dog. Thus he appears to people who take forbidden paths at night or come drunk from the taverns; since such people seldom let anyone pass peacefully, they carry away a thoroughly swollen head from these encounters. Nor do those Kiltgänger go unpunished who usually return very late from their girls on Sunday nights and come running noisily across the bridge. Because he hates shouting, he has even fooled the night watchman who calls the hour. Once, in Lower Lengnau, the watchman caught a loose horse, thinking it was one of the gray horses from the mill there. The millhands who were called out also recognized it as theirs, but just as they took it to put it in the stable, it reared and escaped. The same thing happened there with a foal caught unexpectedly. It allowed itself to be led willingly as far as the roof eaves; there it vanished from their hands, leaving them cheated. In Upper Lengnau it roamed as a calf and in the same way finally tore free from the farmer who had led it by his neckerchief beneath the roof. Two women keeping watch over their laundry by the stream at night saw the spirit approach through the water as a poodle. One took it for an ordinary dog and tried to drive it off; she had to pay for this afterward by never again being able to cross the Surb bridge without meeting the fiend.", "It is a rule that for two weeks before his wedding a bridegroom must not cross the eaves of his home at night. A man of Lengnau paid no heed to this custom and during that period once came home very late from his betrothed. When they parted, the bride had especially warned him to go home only by the usual road, to walk very quietly even there, and not to exchange a word with anyone. Despite this warning, the young daredevil chose not the road but a little footpath along the Surb. There he encountered a man of his own height who blocked the way. The lover did not waste time on questions; he tore a stake from the nearest hedge and struck blindly. But with every blow his opponent’s figure grew taller and its eyes became pure fire, while the young man became as deathly tired as though he had beaten himself. When he finally reached home, he was swollen to nearly a quarter again his size and had become completely bald.
