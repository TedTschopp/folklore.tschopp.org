During the night, he was awakened by the noise, shouting, hallooing, and huzzahs of the Thürst and his followers. He went to the house door to watch the dreadful hunt that usually passed his home. But by the time he managed to open the door in haste, the main procession had already raced past. Only stragglers, the rear guard of the haunting, remained in view; among them, to his amazement, he saw the farmhand to whom he had granted leave the evening before, laboriously dragging himself along. Reflecting upon the strange apparition, the miller returned to rest after the ghostly hour and the haunting had passed, pitying the poor widow who had now undoubtedly lost her husband to the ‘Werbung des Thürst.’ How amazed he was when, early the next morning, he saw the farmhand he thought lost performing his duties again! Admittedly, the man did not seem as fresh as usual and complained greatly of exhaustion and terrible pain in every limb, which felt ‘flayed and battered.’ He could not explain this weariness at all, he said. It was just as though, during the previous night, he had joined the Wild Hunt not merely in a terribly anxious dream but in reality. His astonished master replied that the matter seemed entirely understandable to him; at least, he had not expected the man to return, for he certainly had taken part in the hunt, and the master himself had seen with his own eyes how, one of the last, he limped painfully after the followers. The farmhand, however, would hear nothing of a bodily journey and maintained that he had never left his bed.
