The man spoken of here was not at all miserly or greedy; rather, he had a generous heart toward the poor and toward travelers. At that time, many men and women traveled up and down the country with heavy crosses on their backs, scourging themselves bloody with whips and switches in the deepest repentance for their own sins and those of others. For this reason, they were called the Flagellants. One such man, carrying a very heavy cross, entered the hallway of the house in Krähenmoos one evening and begged for a place to sleep. The rich farm family regarded it as a true blessing and a sign of heavenly favor to have so zealous a penitent safely beneath their roof and to be able to bestow kindnesses upon him. They therefore offered him every good thing they happened to have in store. But he refused the better dishes and, humbly and to the great edification of those standing around, asked only for the poorest fare and a wretched bed. Anything was good enough for him. His bed was prepared on the upper floor of the house. He dragged his heavy cross up there as well. Little by little, everything in the house grew quiet and sank into deep sleep. Only two souls remained awake. The faithful, honest farmhand stayed awake, although he felt an unusual drowsiness; with his own fine, discerning eye, he had looked deep into the stranger's eyes and heart. Toward midnight, he got up and crept quietly to the pilgrim's bedchamber to spy on him. A dim ray of light shone outward through the keyhole, bright enough to reveal a horrifying scene to the watching servant. He saw how the holy symbol of redemption had been misused to serve a wicked man as a hiding place for his instruments of theft and murder. At that very moment, the villain was using magic to make all the inhabitants of the house fall into a deep sleep from which they could not be awakened. He set before him one finger bone from a small child for every person in the house and lit them. All of them burned but one. The murderer immediately guessed who might still be awake and unwilling to sleep, for he too had noticed that the servant's eye had met him not with friendliness and innocence but with suspicion and aversion. \"I will master him easily enough; he is the first one I will do away with,\" said the murderer, taking up a weapon. But the servant outside, whose eyes and ears were keen enough, also had quick arms and legs. In an instant, he flew down the stairs and out of the house to summon the neighbors in Schwandacher to help, knowing that the members of the household who had been enchanted into sleep could not be awakened for the time being. In mortal fear, he also called upon heaven for help. At that very moment, he noticed a little man beside him who took him by the hand. They now moved forward with marvelous speed toward the neighbors, who immediately answered the call for help. Yet no amount of haste was fast enough for the servant. He feared that the dreadful man might already have advanced in his bloody undertaking. On the way back, the servant flew ahead at the hand of the strange little man, as swiftly as thought. Whenever he tried to urge the companions following behind to run faster, the little man always dissuaded him with a peculiar \"Psst!\" They reached the house so quickly that the murderer was only then standing at the top of the stairs, about to hurry down into the rooms to kill. When he saw the men rushing up from the foot of the stairs, he hurled a projectile at the servant, but without success. The men now sprang upon the murderer, seized him, and killed him. They wished to tell the inhabitants of the house what great disaster they had escaped, but they were asleep and could not be awakened. At last, the faithful servant remembered the cause. He went upstairs, where the finger bones were still burning, and extinguished them. In that instant, the spell ended, and everyone awoke." ], "notes": [] }, "legacy_urls": [] } ---
