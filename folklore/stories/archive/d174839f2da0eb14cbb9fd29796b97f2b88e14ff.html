The Gotthard road was not always safe to travel. Soldiers returning home from foreign lands and other work-shy rabble took up the robbers’ trade. The dark Wassnerwald in the municipality of Gurtnellen in particular, stretching almost an hour’s journey between Meitschligen and Wyler, was a feared region. The roaring of the Reuss and the Fellibach drowned out every cry for help and every lament from a victim.", "A robber band of 48 members—exactly as many as there are cards in a Kaiserspielries[1]—occupied a well-hidden cave in the Fellitobel not far from the Fellibrücke and the lonely Güetligaden on the Gotthard road, close beside the rushing Reuss. The members of the band called one another by the names of playing cards: Schallä-Joos, Blass, Schiltä-Sü, Mugg, and so forth. At night by the Fellibrücke they stretched ropes or wire across the road and attached bells to them, or they set traps. They threw their victims’ bodies into the Fellibach or into a Gunten in the raging Reuss—hence, it was said, the names Fellibach, Fellital, and Fellenen.", "Hansli Metzger of Wassen had to carry the most valuable postal consignments over the Gotthard. The robbers had known him for a long time and made many attempts to catch him, but he had always escaped their snares. He only laughed at them, and the band grew utterly enraged with him. He also knew that they had hidden ‘a fearsome heap of money’ in the Güetligaden. One evening, when the band had gone out, he put his little white horse in the Untergaden, whose door at that time faced the road (today it faces the Reuss), and lay down in the Obergaden to taunt the robbers, covering himself with a plank. During the night they returned, but only a very few at a time;[9] in the darkness they took Hansli for one of their own. After all forty-eight had arrived and added their new booty to the old heap of money, they lay down to sleep. Once they were all snoring, Hansli stood up. ‘Who is there?’ one of the robbers called. The fearless Hansli, familiar with the band’s names and habits, answered, ‘D'Schallä-Sü (ds Schallä-Nyni) müess üff ga tschodärä’—‘The Schallä-Sü (the Schallä-Nyni) has to get up to urinate.’ Fortunately that robber was sleeping soundly, or the bold man would have been finished. Hansli then seized the robbers’ money, laid blankets and coats on the floor of the Untergaden and across the Bsetzi, brought out his little horse, and mounted it on the road. ‘Der Hansli Metzger midem grossä Gäldgurt isch da, wenn-er eppis vonem wennt!’—‘Hansli Metzger with the great money belt is here, if you want anything from him!’ he called mockingly up toward the hay door, then galloped away with all his might. The gang awoke and, with Schallä-Joos in the lead, stormed out through the door like a swarm of bees. Schallä-Joos stumbled on the ladder, fell, and broke his right leg, just as one can still see today on the German cards. Yet he pulled himself up and, limping, followed his fellows for quite a distance—‘Syg nu ä ganzä Stuck nachäg'hilpet’—‘He still limped after them for a good long way.’ In the Schluchenkehr below Wassen, beside the ‘marked stone,’ the fastest robbers caught up with Hansli. At the instant when he was about to jump his horse over a Trämel lying in the road, Schiltä-Joos seized the horse’s tail by the hair. Hansli Metzger turned like lightning and, with a powerful stroke of his sword, cut the tail from his own animal. Schiltä-Joos stumbled over the Trämel and fell on his nose, still clutching the horse’s tail tightly in both hands. As an eternal memorial, Schiltä-Joos still holds a little tail in his mouth in the card Ries today.
