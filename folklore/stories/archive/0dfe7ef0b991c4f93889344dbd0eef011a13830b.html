Who here has not heard it while taking a walk across our Aare bridge, up through the vineyards to the so-called Meyer Promenade, and lingering there at the edge of the woods on the foothill to contemplate the lovely landscape at his feet? The land lies all around beneath a blue sky; the clear air allows every firn field and peak far into the snowy mountains to be counted precisely, and every wind is silent. Then, without warning, cannon of the heaviest caliber thunder toward us. Yet no one could name the place, direction, or purpose—where or why the shots are fired. They seem to come nearer, report follows report, yet the pressure in the air that the firing of a gun produces upon those standing closest is entirely absent. Then the cannonade instantly withdraws far along the horizon, and now it sounds only from the Entlebuch mountain chain or from the still more distant mountains of Uri.", "All these shots begin with a short, dull preliminary rumble, followed only afterward by the actual bang, whereas a cannon shot begins with its report and ends with the rolling echo. A well-attested incident from the Swiss Sonderbund War demonstrates it best.", "The battle at Gislikon had been fought in the winter of 1847, and as a result the city of Lucerne had offered its submission to the advancing federal troops. A ceasefire had therefore taken effect on both sides. General Dufour, surrounded by his staff, was on a hill near Lucerne, preparing to direct and oversee the advance of his battalions upon the city. Suddenly a cannon shot was heard far behind them, followed at once by the growl of a second and a third, all seemingly fired from the heaviest guns. The general looked at his companions in astonishment. Peace had already been concluded; the order of the day had already ended all firing. And now, behind the commander, where no enemy remained—on the side of his own men—the cannonade had begun again. How could he not suspect a serious act of insubordination? By chance, however, an Aargau officer familiar with this atmospheric phenomenon of our region was among those around him. He solved the mystery for the astonished general by explaining that these shots were detonations in the upper air, caused by the rapid decomposition of the atmosphere and known locally as the Rothenburger Geschütz. Yet a portion of the men who had heard the firing and likewise recognized it as the familiar natural phenomenon refused for that very reason to believe that the new peace would last. The Rothenburger Geschütz, they said, fires not to usher in peace but to announce war. The troops from Upper Aargau disputed this interpretation. They maintained that their easy marches were now at an end and that they should prepare for a severe storm.
