Once the maid hid in the cellar passage and watched as the woman of the house crept quietly into the kitchen, fetched a little tin from a “Schgäfflein,” and opened it. She took red ointment from the tin, rubbed some of it on the broomstick, closed the tin and quickly put it back in its place, nimbly seated herself on the broomstick, and, with the words “Zum Chämi us und niena-n-â”—out through the chimney and strike nowhere—flew through the chimney and out above the roof. The maid waited and waited until, before daybreak, the woman came safely down through the chimney by the same route, set the broom in the corner, and went to her chamber. “Wenn dô nit öppis derhinder steckt, so weiss i nüt meh, das muess i erdüüsla”—if there is not something behind that, then I know nothing anymore; I must ferret it out—the maid thought, and she too went to rest. One night, when the woman was unwell and the maid had a free hand, she too fetched the little tin, opened it, took some ointment, and did everything exactly as her mistress had done, except that she cried, “Zum Chämi us und überall â”—out through the chimney and strike everywhere. And so it happened: she too flew through the chimney, but struck everywhere, so that she swept its walls clean all over. The broom carried her to the witches' dance on Sträla. Toward daybreak everything scattered again, and she too rode home and down through the chimney, but “überall â”—striking everywhere. She was unwell for a good while afterward and confessed her curiosity to her mistress. The mistress questioned her further, whereupon the maid related how otherwise it had been so lovely on Sträla; only flying through the chimney had not agreed with her.
