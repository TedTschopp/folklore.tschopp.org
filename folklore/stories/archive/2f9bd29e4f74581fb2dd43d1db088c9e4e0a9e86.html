On the second night after the miller’s death, one son saw the dead man swaying back and forth in a terrifying form between the millstones. Nor was that the end of it. The monster disrupted and obstructed every task with the most malicious tricks. Sometimes he diverted the water from the Mühlenkennel so cleverly and secretly that considerable time and effort were required to capture it again and restart the stalled machinery. With the family’s consent, one son now went to Muri and fetched a Benedictine from the abbey there to conjure away the dangerous spirit. The spirit did not yield; on the contrary, he raised the most shameful reproaches against the monk who sought to bind him. A second Banner was therefore summoned from the Capuchin monastery at Laufenburg. When the spirit realized that this man truly possessed the power and right to drive him out, he began to negotiate. He promised to withdraw, but in return demanded a guarantee that each year he might approach his house again by one man’s stride. The Capuchin did not find this entirely objectionable, but restricted it to one Hahnenschritt each year. The spirit was then conjured into a Schoppenglas, and his son carried it out into the forest. He found it so extraordinarily heavy, however, that along the short route he repeatedly had to sit down and rest. Between the mill and the Rhine road, a good quarter-hour from each, lies Wolfesgraben, with a deep cave that has only a very small, hidden entrance. The vessel was sunk inside, and the mouth of the cave was closed with a pane of glass. It is said that from then on a candle could be seen burning inside the cave by day, while the spirit sat beside the opening in the form of a great toad. A vigorous young fellow who often tended cattle near Wolfesgraben did not believe in the monster’s evil tricks and dared to challenge him. He stepped up to the cave and cried, “G'hörst du do inne, wenn du öppis chaist, so chumm usse! Ich nimm's mit dir uf.—If you belong in there, if you can do anything, come out! I’ll take you on.” Nothing appeared, and only a groaning and moaning could be heard below; but the young fellow came home with a swollen head, and the repulsive disfigurement remained with him. An old man still knows how his own father once plowed by moonlight at night near the cave, and how the spirit—a gaunt man dressed in black—came up and followed behind the plow for several furrows.
