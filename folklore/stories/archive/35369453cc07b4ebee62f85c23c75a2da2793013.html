They lacked a means of making the milk curdle without allowing it to sour, for in those days the milk was left to stand until it was completely thick. That produced only sour cheese, which, as everyone knows, does not taste especially good. The wild little men, also called Fänggen, did understand the art of “sweet cheesemaking,” however, and one of our ancestors learned it from one of them. Once, at the Maiensäss of Schuders, a wild Fänggenmannli lived on familiar terms with the Senn and received all manner of presents and gifts from him. One evening the Senn said that the following day he had to carry butter down to his people in the village, and he asked the little being to make cheese for him. The Fängge accepted the proposal, for he intended to give him a demonstration of his natural science for once. The Senn went into the village, and the little being made cheese. How astonished the Senn was when he returned that evening, tasted the cheese made by the Fängge, and found it as sweet as fresh butter. For a long time he tried to persuade the Fänggenmannli to tell him how one could make “sweet cheese,” but our little mountain being could not be persuaded. Then the Senn resorted to cunning. Several weeks later, when the Fängge entered the hut one morning, he said with a beaming face, “Now I too know how to make sweet cheese.” The wild little fellow flared up: “If you have made sweet cheese, then you must also have had a stomach.” Not a flicker of his expression revealed that the Senn now knew the secret the Fängge had always withheld from him. He experimented with the Gizimagen; the attempt succeeded, and from that time on he could make the finest sweet cheese.
