A young man from Gurtnellen was walking home from Amsteg at night. In those days there was still the old, narrow pack road. As he approached the Fellitobel, he heard music, ‘äs Tanzspill,’ and as soon as he rounded the corner and could see the little bridge in the Tobel, he perceived a bright light beneath it. In that light a number of black cats were dancing—not on a floor, but above the water of the Fellibach flowing under the bridge. A large black cat with a stumpy tail played a Handorgelen for them. Whenever it paused, the dancers called, ‘Stumper, spill is wider einä-n-üff!’—‘Stumper, play us another one!’ The traveler stood spellbound, watching the performance until the whole enchantment suddenly vanished. He then continued on his way, not without reflecting on what he had seen. The next morning at Kalatzen, the master's black cat came over. It too had a stumpy tail, and now it was clear to the young man: ‘That is Stumper, who played beneath the bridge yesterday evening.’ He told the master everything and jokingly asked, ‘Soll-er-es firha?’—whether he should confront him with it. The master had no objection, and the young man asked the cat, ‘So Stumper, wo bisch nächtig gsy?’—‘Well, Stumper, where were you last night?’—‘I ha miässä fort’—‘I had to go away.’—‘Jä, dä hesch-di mein-i luschtig gmacht und bisch bim Tanz gsy und hesch üffg'spillt!’—‘Well then, I think you enjoyed yourself, went to the dance, and played the music!’ At these words the cat vanished and left behind a terrible stench.", "Joh. Every Saturday evening it used to go out and return only in the morning; on Sunday, by contrast, it surrendered to sweet sleep behind the stove or in some other warm corner and let the mice celebrate. This neglect of duty by the household animal eventually attracted attention, and the farmer asked, ‘Washed ächt äu ysärä Stimper, dass der all Samschtigabed fortgaht und der ganz Sunntig üss eißter schlaft?’—‘Whatever is the matter with our Stimper, that he goes away every Saturday evening and then sleeps straight through the whole Sunday?’ At last it was to come to light. One Saturday evening it happened that the Rothüser family's servant, who tended the livestock on the Schattdorferberg, was carrying milk down to the valley floor. A light burned in the little house in the Rämeli—or Kellerberg. Four Meitli lived there who had the reputation of being old witches. From time to time young men also came to them z'Gass, but most came only once or twice; the maidens' peculiar behavior did not suit them. When the servant saw the little light, he thought they were holding Stubeten again, went over, and peered curiously through the little window. An unexpected spectacle met his eyes. Four cats were dancing as though possessed in the room, and on the parlor table—or the stove—sat the fifth, a white nightcap on its Grind, playing music for the merry company on a bone it used like a mouth organ. After a while the musician took the bone from its mouth and handed it to one of the dancers with the words, ‘Ich mag nimmä-n-orgälä, Stimper, tüe dü!’—‘I cannot play anymore, Stimper; you do it!’ The servant's eyes opened wide. Sure enough, the cat that had been asked took the peculiar Mülorgäli and sprang onto the table, and by its stumpy tail the young man now recognized Stimper. Now he knew why the cat went out every Saturday evening and slept on Sunday. ‘Wer häd äu das 'tänkt!’—‘Who would ever have thought it!’ When the family sat at the table eating breakfast the next morning, the cat too jumped onto the bench and sat beside the servant. Coaxingly and begging, it rubbed its round little head against his elbow and purred contentedly.
