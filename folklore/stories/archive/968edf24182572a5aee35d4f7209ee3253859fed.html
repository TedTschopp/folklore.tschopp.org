One evening a foreign gentleman came into the hut and asked whether he might board with them and stay overnight for a day or two. Yes, the Senn said, he certainly could, provided he was content with the food and the Gliger they had. There was room enough. The gentleman said that yes, yes, he was content; they need make no special arrangements for him. So he stayed and spent the night there. When they asked where he came from, he said, ‘From Venice.’ Early the next morning he rose, took a sack on his back, and went away, remaining out all day. In the evening he returned with the sack full of stones, showed them to the men, and said that he intended to make a handsome fortune from them in Venice. That night the Senn thought, ‘Ah, you will take a couple of those stones from him; he will not notice, and later you too will go with them to Venice and see what you can make from them!’ Without further ado he rose and set two or three of the smaller ones aside. In the morning the stranger looked over his stones once more, but gave no sign that he noticed anything. Then he paid the Älpler properly and fairly, took his leave, thanked them, and departed, presumably for Venice. In autumn, after they had descended from the Alp and the main autumn work was over, the Senn thought, ‘Now you too will go toward Venice with your stones,’ and set out. When he entered the city of Venice, someone called down to him from a tall, handsome house. He looked up and immediately recognized the foreign gentleman who had stayed with him on the Alp that summer. ‘So, now you have come with your stolen stones?’ the gentleman called to him. The Senn was thoroughly dismayed, as one can imagine. But the gentleman was entirely friendly and told him to come up; no harm would happen to him—on the contrary, he would meet with nothing but kindness. At last he entered the house and went upstairs to the gentleman. The latter was exceedingly friendly and again told him not to be afraid; the men on the Alp had been friendly to him too. He set food and drink before the Senn and urged him to help himself. Well, he did not need much urging; he ate and drank heartily and with a good appetite. Then the gentleman told him to take his stones to a certain house and say there that they should give him what the stones were worth. He went, found the house, and received an enormous heap of money for them. He returned with it to the gentleman, who asked whether he would now like to be home soon. ‘Indeed,’ he replied, ‘the sooner, the better.’ The gentleman took him to the very top of the house and opened the door of a chamber. It was pitch-dark inside, and all the shutters were closed. The gentleman told him to go in, remain there in the darkness, and keep walking around and around. But he must never stop throughout the whole night—he must keep walking! Only when he thought morning might be drawing near should he open the shutters. The Senn went in; the gentleman closed the chamber door and left. All night long he walked continually around the room without stopping. In the morning, when he thought day was breaking, he opened the shutters. From his own little house he was looking straight down upon the little village of Wassen, and at Wassen the prayer bell was just ringing.—That journey had been easy and swift indeed!", "Well, now of course he was a rich man.
