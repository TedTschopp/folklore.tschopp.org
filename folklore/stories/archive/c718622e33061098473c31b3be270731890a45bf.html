The entire surrounding region knows that in former times little men three feet tall lived here. At that time, however, these caves were halls full of splendor. Golden chandeliers stood inside, upon each of which eight candles burned at once throughout the day; the walls were made of glass and reflected the lights’ glow. Yet despite this magnificence, each of the little men had in turn to perform the most menial household tasks: sweeping, baking, dusting the chandeliers, and, during harvest time, even stripping the ears of grain from the stalks in the field and laboriously beating and threshing them with stones inside the cave. Their Wähen, delicious cakes made from the very thinnest dough, were especially famous. Whenever a farmer plowed the land into whose ripe grain they had previously gone, they came to him in the field and gratefully placed such a fine baked cake upon the Höchli of his plow. This pleased the plowman very well, and he ate it without any misgivings. He won the war for them and became dear and valued among them. At last, they reminded him that he now had to return above to the world, took leave of him, and gave him a wagon full of gold. When the man of Gippingen returned to his village with it, his relatives had long since died, and none of his neighbors recognized him. He himself was as old as though he had spent many hundreds of years inside the mountain.
