Whenever the mother tells of the monster, she draws the children's attention to its power to transform itself into every possible shape and admonishes the little ones, if they have to fetch coffee from the shopkeeper at night and therefore pass that Trotte, to cross themselves diligently so that the spirit does them no harm. “I speak,” she says, “from a sad experience! There he saw the dreaded spirit approaching in gigantic form, holding a spear, a Weidsack strapped around him, and a Dreiröhrenhut upon his head. The sight was so terrifying that Father fell unconscious for hours. In summer it brazenly takes ripe cherries from the trees, in autumn it shakes down the pears, and not infrequently it even fetches the gunsmith's Christmas nut from the fruit chamber. What it does not steal, it ruins. It throws tiles from roofs, strangles young chickens outside the window, snaps graft shoots, smashes panes, makes pigs ill, and torments the cattle so badly that a ram has to be kept in every stable. The Unterdörfer once tried to remedy the evil and have the spirit exorcised by Kapuziner. But they could do nothing to it, no matter how fervently everyone prayed or how well they caught it. Four men were already carrying it into the forest inside a copper kettle upon a Mistbahre. The Kapuziner followed behind, praying. Then one bearer began to groan softly beneath the ever-heavier load: “May the . . . take you!” At that, as if seized by a storm wind, the kettle flew into the air—where to, no one knows.
