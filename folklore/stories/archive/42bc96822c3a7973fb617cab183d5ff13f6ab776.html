The city of Rheinfelden was stormed three times, and the Poor Clares’ convent at Olsberg in the Fricktal was destroyed for the third time. A charcoal burner sat despairing in the forest before his Meiler, considering where he should flee. “The little village of Magden below has already burned; why continue burning charcoal here?” he said. “No more than that stone hill will turn to gold will my bitter labor save me from starvation.”", "At these words, strange sounds suddenly rose directly from the depths of the hill upon which he sat. Before he had properly collected his thoughts, three snow-white maidens stood before him—or rather floated around the charcoal pile without a single black speck adhering to their magnificent cloaks. They had flowers in their hair and little golden rods in their hands. One pointed hers toward the summit of the limestone rock, and it immediately opened gently into a great passage. They led the charcoal burner down into a broad hall with golden walls and offered him the key with which he was to open the treasure chests standing all around. The poor man did not know what was happening. Half from heartfelt astonishment and half from Christian fear for his soul, he began to cry, “All good spirits . . .!” In a flash he felt himself whirled upward and thrown into the sunlight beneath the old oaks on the hill, while bitter lamenting and wailing sounded from the ground. On the plateau opposite the village quarry, extending downward as far as Augst, a basin-shaped hollow runs for several thousand feet, where numerous quail are found among riverside willows. The singing Wachletä-Jumpfere are said to have lived there, and our charcoal burner is said to have dwelt on this height. Once he still lay sleepless upon his sack of leaves when the Magden church tower had already struck one. Then sneezing began outside his hut. “God help you,” he said, “God help you!” He said it perhaps thirty times without the sneezing outside ceasing. “If God does not help you,” he said at last, “then may the Devil.” The sneezing stopped, but now thunder and crashing filled the forest until morning. When the man stepped outside early, he saw that his entire Meiler had sunk except for one basket of charcoal, while abundant water welled up in its place. Had he not been a fool, he would have carried the basket of charcoal neatly into the house; instead, in anger, he threw it into the water. The water still flows today, but no one drinks from it, for otherwise it causes goiters.
