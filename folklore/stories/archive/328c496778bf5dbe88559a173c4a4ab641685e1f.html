The first cattle pestilence raged so severely that one morning nine cows were found lying dead together in the pasture. A Capuchin from the Lucerne region, probably coming from Schüpfheim by way of Sörenberg, was summoned to bind the evil. He came, banished it beneath the earth into a pit, and closed the opening with a piece of wood. He also commanded that every year thereafter, on Saint John's Night, half a day's yield from the cows then summering on the Alps of Bohl and Ällgäu be made into cheese and Zieger and distributed among the poor of the surrounding area. He promised that these Alps would be spared the cattle plague for as long as the command was faithfully obeyed and the hole remained closed.", "According to others, the Oberland miracle doctor Bühlfritzli placed the pestilence inside the wood. But the mysterious timber was later felled, and the plague returned. Bühlfritzli, the son of the man just mentioned, was now fetched. In 1800 or 1801, he secretly placed the plague into a piece of wood so that no one knew of it except a few herdsmen. He also had brushwood and Reckholder gathered into a great pile, set it alight, and made the cattle walk around the fire. The smoke did not rise but spread across the cattle and enveloped them completely. They were thereby freed entirely from the plague. The first event gave rise to a custom that still exists: the Alps of Bohl and Ällgäu turn the milk drawn on the morning of July 4 into cheese and Zieger. On the fifth, they carry the unsalted cheese and Zieger to the Twirrispeicher and distribute it there among the poor. This is called Sankt Johannsen, or the Sankt Johannser, because it takes place on Saint John's Day according to the old calendar.
