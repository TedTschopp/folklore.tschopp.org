It is a pity that it is not more widely known. It consists of three sections extending one behind the other in a straight line into the mountain, all of the same shape: high and broad at the entrance, then closing symmetrically and narrowing inward. The side walls and the vault-like ceiling are so uniform and regular that one might think the grottoes, or halls as we might call them, had been hewn by human hands. The first hall has a great portal; it is about 8 meters long, 3 meters wide at the front and 1 ½ meters wide at the rear, 7 meters high at the entrance and 3 meters high at the exit. Here one passes as though through a door into the second hall, which occupies about one third the space of the first. The third section can be entered only in a stooped, kneeling posture. It is smaller than the middle one by another half. At its end is a vertical opening ¾ of a meter in diameter. Until now, no one has dared descend there to investigate the mountain's interior. In ancient times, a fierce dragon is said to have lived in this grotto, from which the cave received the name Drachenloch. But after a long time, the dragon is said to have grown bored in its castle and dared the bold flight across the Tamina to the Calanda. From Vättis one can see, in a sheer high rock face, the hole into which it flew. But it found no ground or footing inside the mountain and then fell many thousands of feet and perished miserably there.\n‘Oberländer Anzeiger.’" ], "notes": [ "Swiss term “Drachenloch”: literally ‘dragon's hole,’ the local name of the high grotto above Vättis; the legend explains the name by making the cave the dragon's former mountain dwelling before its fatal flight to the Calanda." ] }, "legacy_urls": [] } ---
