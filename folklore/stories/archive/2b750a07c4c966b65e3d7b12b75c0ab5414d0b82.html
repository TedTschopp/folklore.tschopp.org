Because he stood in the highest favor with the abbot of Muri, no one dared accuse him, and his outrages grew ever greater.", "Beyond Schongau on Lucerne soil lived a pious woman who, in her old age, bequeathed her fine farm to Muri Abbey. This greatly pleased the greedy Stiefelireiter. He immediately rode up to the farm and inspected it. Then he entered the house, where the old woman was just eating her soup, and explained that the property would not be very useful to the monastery so long as it remained interrupted by a small intervening holding that necessarily belonged with the complete farm. For the good of the monastery and the salvation of her own soul, the woman should therefore add a clause to her will ensuring that this little holding too fell to the abbey as part of the inheritance. The woman grew so indignant that she rose from her soup and curtly showed Stiefeli the door. That very smallholding, which consisted of only a few meadows and fields, belonged to her brother’s daughter, who lived there in a thatched cottage. Precisely out of the thoughtful intention that this abandoned woman should continue to be protected in her poor cottage, the woman of Schongau had made the monastery the sole neighboring landowner of her relative in the will. But Stiefeli knew how to help himself. He took possession of the parchment roll containing the Schongau bequest, and because he had successfully practiced imitating every possible handwriting from an early age, he added in a skillfully disguised hand between the text and the signature of the will these further words: \"together with the cottage and the property hitherto occupied by my brother’s daughter.\" After the benefactress’s death, a legal dispute soon arose over the falsified document, but Stiefeli ended it by swearing upon the disputed property that \"as surely as his Schöpfer and Richter are above him, so surely does he stand on the soil and ground of the monastery.\" Scarcely had the oath left his mouth when he cried out in pain and rolled about the meadow in the convulsions of death.
