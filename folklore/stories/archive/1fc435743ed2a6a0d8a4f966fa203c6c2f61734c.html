Without ceasing, they gnawed at the rocks and caused scree and boulders to plunge into the valley. To prevent a greater disaster, the people turned to their devout priest to banish the spirits. Reluctantly he accepted the difficult assignment, but he wished to try. For three days he prepared himself through fasting and severe acts of penance. He even made his bed for three nights in the churchyard among the graves of the dead, and when he climbed the mountain to the spirits, he took off his stockings and placed broken shells in his shoes. He found them, to be sure, but they mocked and ridiculed him. Had he perhaps come to join their company, since he was one of their kind—a thief? The bewildered priest, who had a good conscience, would not admit this. But they reminded him that once, while climbing the mountain tired, he had taken a Rebstichel from a vineyard to use in his hand and had not yet returned it. The priest now recognized his fault and went back to make restitution.", "When the priest appeared again, the spirits could find no further objection except: ‘Mârait n’est jamais bon foin!—Sedge’—an allusion to his name—‘is never good hay!’ ‘But sedge is good for obstinate mules,’ the priest replied.
