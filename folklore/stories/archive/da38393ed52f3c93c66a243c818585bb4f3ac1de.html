They withdrew from worldly traffic and were generally considered shy of people. They gladly helped quiet, upright people in house and field and relieved them of many a task. They soon established friendly relations with Saint Beatus. They were exceedingly grateful that he had driven the dragon from the cave, for it had terrified them too. Now, they could once again emerge unhindered into daylight from the interior of the fissured mountain where they lived, following the rock cleft of the Beatenbach. They provided for the apostle's bodily sustenance and brought him dry wood for fuel so that smoke would not torment him too badly in his cave. They fetched water for him from the murmuring brook nearby; when the weather was especially unpleasant, they slipped for this purpose through the narrow rock hole in the northwestern corner of his cave to draw from the rock-born spring deep inside the mountain. From the Flühe, they brought him Gemsmilch and fine Käslein. Indeed, they even led chamois to him so he could treat them like tame goats. East of the entrance to his hermitage, he made goat or chamois stalls in the small grottoes. With great care, the little dwarfs tended and milked his domestic animals. They also helped him plant and care for all kinds of fruit trees below his cave. They gently gathered the ripe fruit and brought it into his cave. But Beatus used very little for himself. He carried the juicy fruit to the sick as refreshment. The healing herbs from his little garden also served as medicine for all kinds of sufferers in the region. The little dwarfs brought him many a rare plant with healing properties down from the high mountain peaks.
