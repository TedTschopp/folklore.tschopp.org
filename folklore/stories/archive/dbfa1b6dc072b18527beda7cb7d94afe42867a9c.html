His mother told him that it was a sad story, but that she would tell it to him: he had lost his life through the aunt’s fault, in the following way. “Your father was a large, strong, courageous man and an experienced hunter. He kept his cattle, which he fed himself, on the estate called ‘Schichieu,’ where he often went fox hunting and for that purpose always had a loaded firearm nearby. One evening a large fox came and walked slowly back and forth before the stable as though it wished to take a stroll; the following evening it came again just as your father was emerging from the barn with hay. He went into the stable, fetched the gun, and took aim, but the shot would not go off. The fox then stood still on its hind legs and rubbed its nose with its front paws. Angered, your father went into the stable to fetch another loaded Stutzer, which he also kept ready, and took aim again. But now, instead of fleeing, the fox came ever closer—so close that it could say in your father’s ear, ‘Aim well, my Kaspar.’ Frightened, your father let the gun fall, for he had recognized the voice of the aunt, who had transformed herself into a fox.” After this the animal vanished. Deathly pale, the father then came home and went to bed, and only with difficulty could I learn the cause of his terror. Although he was the strongest man for miles around, he died after three days as a result of the fright the aunt had given him. — Yes, believe me, my son, the aunt was a witch; for when you were baptized and your father and I, many relatives and friends, Götti and Gotte, were gathered for a meal, and everyone sat happily at the table with our glasses of wine, the aunt too entered with majestic steps.
