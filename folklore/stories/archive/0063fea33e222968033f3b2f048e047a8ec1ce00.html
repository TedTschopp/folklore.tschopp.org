In a house opposite, he saw that every window was lit, and he heard merry but old-fashioned dance tunes being played. \"What is that?\" he said to himself. \"Has the devil got into the young people, that they are dancing here in secret at so late an hour, and during the Ember Days at that? I thought I was entirely alone on the Eggen, and now I find a secret company of dancers there!—First I will prepare myself some supper—and then see who is making merry over there.\" After eating some supper, he crept unseen as far as the front door of the house, which stood half open. So as not to be heard, he went quietly in on tiptoe as far as the parlor door; it too was slightly open. Through this opening he saw lights on the table and, at one corner of it, the fiddler and several other people; but everyone was dressed in a thoroughly old-fashioned manner. The dancers whom he could see circling around were also mostly in old-fashioned dress; at the same time he heard a strange tinkling like small pieces of ice. When he looked more closely at the dancers, he noticed, to no small astonishment, that the men and women had what looked like little icicles and pieces of ice on their clothes, and that the fingers on their hands also looked like icicles. At the very moment he noticed this, he saw a young woman whose clothing seemed utterly familiar to him. \"My God!\" he thought. \"She resembles my recently deceased sweetheart, my unforgettable dance partner, as one drop of water resembles another; what kind of company is this?\" And just as he thought this, she turned around and beckoned with her hand for him to come inside. Now he recognized her completely: \"It was—Emma—his deceased sweetheart!\" He turned ice-cold with terror and shivered as if a tubful of cold water had been poured over him. He hurried back to his dwelling as fast as his trembling legs could carry him, locked it securely, and hastened to bed. Although he had wrapped himself well in the bedclothes, a violent feverish chill shook him, and sleep was out of the question. He may have remained in this state until about midnight—then the front door of the house opened, and there was already a knock at the parlor door. He hid his head beneath the covers, for he had no desire to call, \"Come in.\" Then that door opened as well, and despite his fear, he dared to peer out a little from beneath the covers. It was the figure of a woman, as far as he could judge in the darkness. \"Emma!\" he thought, his heart pounding, and hid himself in the bedclothes again. Then he heard the tinkling of ice, just as in the dance hall, only now it was drawing closer to his bed. His fear reached its height; the spirit climbed onto his bed and even lay down beside him.—A faint cry of terror rose from his breast: \"Jesus, Mary, and Joseph! Who are you?\"—It seemed to him that an ice-cold shadow bent over him and touched his lips. The spirit had now been addressed and, according to folk belief, had the right to draw upon his breath and speak with him. But the living are also said to lose all fear of the dead after the first words are spoken to them. Of the long conversation carried on with the spirit until the morning prayer bell, the young man is said to have revealed only this much: the first thing the spirit said to him was the question, \"Do you know me?\" And he answered, \"Yes—you are Emma!\"—\"Yes, I am Emma, your former sweetheart. Emma was his sole thought throughout his life.
