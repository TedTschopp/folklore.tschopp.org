Our Schmied was now on his latest return journey to Laufenburg and once again reached Sisselen late. It was a moonlit spring night, and every leaf on the ground could be distinguished. At the crossroads by the village, he saw a whole company of frogs ceaselessly hopping about one another in such a narrow space that they seemed to have been trained for it. Strange as thoughts may run, the great number of animals within such a definite boundary struck him as peculiar; but only now did he also remember the Basel gift that he had promised yet again and forgotten yet again. Once more, night had come much too soon for him, and he knew his wife did not make idle threats. It therefore seemed a very amusing idea to put half a dozen of these frogs into his sack and present them at home in place of the market trinket. That, he thought with a smile, would cause such a fright that in future he would certainly be spared every costly demand. No sooner thought than done. Six fat specimens, easily caught, were soon inside a Zwilchsacke, which he tied firmly and placed in the wagon basket.", "Late though he came home that day, his wife was friendly this time and called down from the window to ask whether he had brought what he promised. ‘Why, certainly,’ came the answer. ‘Just come down and fetch it yourself; it lies safely in the sack at the back of the wagon basket.’ The woman ran up and down through the house, then tried by the upstairs light to open the tightly tied sack, while her husband kept busy with the load below so he could avoid the expected uproar. Now the sack was open. Curiosity left her no time to reach inside first, and she shook it out as it was upon the large table. What delight! She had never hoped for such costly market goods: half a dozen weighty gold coins rolled onto the table, gleaming as though they had just come brand-new from the mint. While the woman gazed at them, the carter entered the room and could scarcely trust his wife, let alone himself, when he saw and learned into what Goldfüchse the ugly toads had changed. He now received a complete pardon, and my grandmother herself, who knew Schmied and his wife well, told me that this peace between the spouses endured. (I. A.
