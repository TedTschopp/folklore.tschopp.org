Popular belief soon had the hated man walking again, for the papers he left behind provided ample proof that he had cheated widows and orphans to the very end. People claimed to have seen him travel down the bell wire from the top floor to the bottom. Country folk who gathered in the town on Sundays could stand before that house for hours, staring at it with secret dread. Because the heirs could find no buyer, they had a Capuchin friar come from Olten to bless the ghost away. He succeeded in banishing the spirit to the town forest called the Gönhard. A stone table stands there, and all sorts of stories are told about its origin. Some claim that the helvetische Regierung—the Helvetic government—had it erected while it resided in Aarau so that its members could sometimes eat supper outdoors. Older folk belief, however, sees it as the meeting place of witches, Besenreiterinnen, and the Devil's sisters, who feast and dance there at midnight. The Capuchin banished the usurer's spirit to that place. Before long, however, a woodcutter saw him rummaging in money at the table and wailing enough to turn one's heart. The relatives once again became the subject of public gossip. On the Capuchin's advice, a mattress was then carried out at night and laid in the forest scrub so that the spirit might rest more quietly. Not long afterward, a poor little farmer from Suhr came that way, happened to see the mattress, and happily loaded it up. Yet it grew heavier the farther he carried it. Driven by an inner feeling, he soon dragged it back to its former place and made off. Meanwhile, a foreign goldsmith had bought the unoccupied house in town for a song and is said to have found a considerable sum of money while digging up the cellar. From that time onward, the house was completely quiet.
