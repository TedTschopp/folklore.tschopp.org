As so often happens, the girl was on the point of giving her affection forever, as she thought, to a young man. But the Erdmännchen came and showed himself very saddened by this. She would not fare well with that man; he was not what one could wish; and to him the little man could not entrust the important gift that he would so gladly give her suitor for her welfare. The girl was moved, abandoned this choice, and gave her hand to a man who fully possessed the Erdmännchen’s confidence. And what did the little man give him? Alas, only a tinderbox consisting of steel and flint. How foolish! No, not so foolish. ‘Look,’ said the little man, ‘this is a very important tinderbox. You must care for it as you would the greatest treasure, but you may never use it except in the direst need. Then, when you strike it, someone behind your back will immediately ask, “What do you want?” Without looking around, you answer by stating your wish—and it is fulfilled.’ He was not permitted to tell his wife anything about the gift. Very well. When, in the next merry month of May, the young husband spied the loveliest cliff flowers high on a rocky ledge, an irresistible urge seized him to pick them and bring them to his wife as a greeting. A bold and skillful climber, he ventured to the dangerous place and secured the bouquet, but now the rock crumbled away. Only a bush, to which he still clung with one hand, allowed him to delay his fall for a few seconds. The tinderbox? Yes, he suddenly remembered it. His free hand drew it from his pocket and, with difficulty, managed to push the stone between two fingers of the other hand and strike fire. The instant this was done, someone behind his back asked his wish. No sooner was it spoken than it was fulfilled, freeing the poor man from all mortal fear. The noble tinderbox then lay for a long time, half forgotten. Once, the wife fell ill. The Erdmännchen came and brought medicines. But who knows what dark mood possessed the husband: he half distrusted the remedy. For that reason, the woman did not recover, and her condition worsened by the hour. She already lay at death’s door. The man was inconsolable and sobbed: ‘Is there no herb left growing on God’s earth that can help?’ ‘Fool—your tinderbox!’ he suddenly thought and said, half vexed that it had occurred to him only now. He reached into his pocket—nothing was there! It now struck him that his mistrust of the good Erdmännchen might well be to blame for the disappearance of the means of rescue in his direst need. He repented bitterly before God. ‘Oh, only this one more time!’ he cried to his unseen, offended friend, who truly relented and restored the tinderbox.
